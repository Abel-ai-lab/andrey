# Multi-domain data

## TL;DR

- Multi-domain data pool rows from several environments, such as sites, batches, or the conditions
  of an experiment, that share one causal structure and differ in some mechanisms. With each row's
  environment as one more variable, a method can tell a dependence that a shift creates from an
  edge, and the shifts can direct edges that one environment leaves undirected.
- Andrey has CD-NOD and FCI-JCI for these data, and GES, hill climbing, Tabu search, GFCI, BOSS,
  GRaSP, BOSS-FCI, and GRaSP-FCI take the environment too, as an experimental option. Each lists the
  environment's edges, and CD-NOD, GES, hill climbing, Tabu search, BOSS, and GRaSP also list the
  variables the environments change.
- FCI-JCI ties pcalg, its authors' implementation, in all 12 comparisons and is 4 to 79 times
  faster. BOSS-FCI and GRaSP-FCI each tie Tetrad 7.6.10, their authors' current implementation, in
  72 of 73 comparisons and are better in one, on data with hidden common causes and no `env`.

---

Data rarely come from one place. Hospitals, labs, batches, seasons, and the conditions of an
experiment each shift some part of a system and leave the rest alone. Pooled without a record of
where each row came from, the shifts can mislead a method; recorded, they can help it.

## Why pool environments

Take two variables with $x \to y$, a linear effect, and Gaussian noise. From the rows of one
environment, $x \to y$ and $y \to x$ fit the data alike, so PC and GES leave the edge undirected.
Now a second environment shifts the mean of $x$, and you record each row's environment as one more
variable, $C$, the environment index.

- Nothing in the data causes $C$: an experimenter, a site, or a time period sets it. So every edge
  at $C$ has its arrowhead at the data variable, here $C \to x$.
- $C$ and $y$ are dependent through $x$ and independent given $x$. With $C \to x$ fixed, the only
  graph that fits is $C \to x \to y$, so the index directs the edge. Had the second environment
  shifted $y$ instead, $C$ and $x$ would be independent and $y$ a collider, $C \to y \leftarrow x$,
  with the same direction.

Pooling without $C$ goes wrong in another way. When one environment shifts both $x$ and an
unrelated $z$, the pooled rows show $x$ and $z$ moving together, and a method draws an edge between
them. With $C$ among the variables, $x$ and $z$ are independent given $C$, and the edge goes, while
the mechanisms that stay the same are fit on all the rows at once.

The variables $C$ points into are the ones whose mechanism, the distribution given their causes,
changes across the environments. Their descendants change too, but only through them.

Joint Causal Inference (Mooij, Magliacane, and Claassen, 2020) applies this to any method that takes
such background knowledge: add the environment as a context variable that no data variable causes,
and run the method on the pooled data. CD-NOD (Huang et al., 2020) does it for PC.

## Multi-domain data in Andrey

Every method here takes `env`, each row's environment, in the forms the
[interventional methods](20261105-andrey-0-14-0.md#interventional-data-in-andrey) take it: a label
per row, the name of a DataFrame column, or each environment's rows as a table apart. They read it
as the environment index and report its edges in the result's `metadata`; CD-NOD, GES, hill
climbing, Tabu search, BOSS, and GRaSP also report the variables it changes there:

```python
# "site" is a column of text labels, which is not a variable. The default Fisher z test and BIC
# score would read the labels as numbers, so these calls read them as categories.
out = andrey.cdnod(data, "site", indep_test="cond_gauss")
out.metadata["changing_variables"]  # the variables whose mechanism changes across the sites
out.metadata["index_edges"]  # the index's edges, such as ["x", "directed"]
out = andrey.boss(data, "site", score_func="cond_gauss", categorical=[])  # BOSS, same index
out = andrey.fci_jci(data, "site", indep_test="cond_gauss", confounded_index=False)
```

| Method | Search | Hidden common causes | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| CD-NOD | PC's tests | not allowed | CPDAG, index edges, changing variables |
| FCI-JCI | FCI's tests | allowed | PAG, index edges |
| BOSS-FCI, GRaSP-FCI | a BOSS or GRaSP search, then tests | allowed | PAG, index edges |
| GFCI | a GES search, then tests | allowed | PAG, index edges |
| GES, hill climbing, Tabu search | a score search over graphs | not allowed | CPDAG, index edges, changing variables |
| BOSS, GRaSP | a search over variable orders | not allowed | CPDAG, index edges, changing variables |

CD-NOD (Huang et al., 2020) runs PC with the index as one more variable that can only be a cause.
Every edge it keeps at the index points into a changing variable.

FCI-JCI (Mooij, Magliacane, and Claassen, 2020) runs FCI the same way, so it allows hidden common
causes; it is new in this release, and supported. `confounded_index` says whether a hidden variable
may cause both the environment and a data variable. `True`, the default, leaves the index's end of
an edge open until a rule orients it; `False`, for an environment set from outside as in an
experiment, points every edge the index keeps out of it. Each index edge is `directed`,
`partially_directed`, or `bidirected`.

BOSS-FCI and GRaSP-FCI (Ramsey, Andrews, and Spirtes, 2025) start from the CPDAG of a BOSS or GRaSP
search, remove with tests the edges that hidden common causes explain, and orient the rest with
FCI's rules. They follow the steps of the paper's Algorithm 5, and both are new in this release,
and supported. With `env`, the index leads every variable order of their search, and their tests
and rules treat it as FCI-JCI does. `edge_removal="triangles"` tests only the edges that lie in a
triangle of the CPDAG, the rule of Section 5 of the GFCI paper (Ogarrio, Spirtes, and Ramsey,
2016); GFCI takes it too.

GES, hill climbing, Tabu search, BOSS, and GRaSP add the index as a variable that no data variable
causes and search over the graphs that keep every edge at it pointing out. GFCI does the same and
runs its tests and rules as FCI-JCI does. In these six methods, and in BOSS-FCI and GRaSP-FCI,
`env` is experimental. Without `env`, every method returns what it returned before.

## Sachs's nine conditions

Sachs and colleagues (2005) measured 11 proteins and phospholipids in single human immune cells:
853 under a general stimulation, the observational cells, and the rest under eight more
conditions, each with a reagent that stimulates or inhibits part of the signaling network. The
known network has 17 arcs, a consensus that Mooij, Magliacane, and Claassen (2020) note is
contested. `load_dataset("sachs", conditions=True)` loads all 7,466 cells with each cell's
condition as `env`.

The condition codes 0 to 8 have no order, so the example reads them as categories, with the
conditional Gaussian test and score. The default Fisher z test and BIC score read `env` as a number,
and text labels as their codes 0, 1, ... in sorted order: they find a change in a mean that follows
the codes' order, and recoding the conditions can change the result. The experimenters set each
cell's condition, so nothing hidden causes it, and `confounded_index=False` tells FCI-JCI and
BOSS-FCI so. The example fits the measurements as they load, without a log transform.

From the 853 observational cells, PC keeps 8 edges, all in the consensus network. Pooled, the nine
conditions give PC almost nine times the rows and 25 edges, but only 12 of them are in the
consensus network. The conditions shift many of the measured variables at once, and in the pooled
rows those variables move together.

With `env`, every method in the example keeps 9 or 10 edges, 8 or 9 of them in the consensus
network. GES and BOSS each find 9 arcs of the consensus network, one more than PC on the
observational cells. Each method keeps an edge from the index to all 11 variables, and CD-NOD lists
all 11 as changing: every measured variable changes across the conditions.

That is also why the index directs nothing here. The index directs an edge when it touches one end
and not the other; here it touches both. GES and BOSS leave all 10 edges undirected. FCI-JCI and
BOSS-FCI fully direct none of theirs, though colliders among the measured variables put arrowheads
on 6 and 2 of them; CD-NOD directs 4 the same way.

## Checked against the authors' code

We compared the three methods this release makes supported with their authors' implementations,
on data simulated the way their papers simulate it. Only FCI-JCI's data have environments;
BOSS-FCI's and GRaSP-FCI's have one environment and hidden common causes.

- FCI-JCI ties pcalg 2.7-12's FCI-JCI in all 12 comparisons on the JCI paper's simulation at 4 and
  10 variables, under two assumption sets at alpha 0.01 and 0.05. It is 4.0 to 4.6 times faster at
  4 variables and 72 to 79 times faster at 10. At 100 variables with 100 rows per regime, every
  pcalg fit on the two datasets of a dry run ran past 1,800 s, so we ran Andrey alone there; each of
  Andrey's fits took under half a second and made fewer errors than the empty graph.
- BOSS-FCI ties Tetrad 7.6.10's BOSS-FCI in 72 of 73 comparisons and is better in one, at 100
  variables. The data follow the BOSS-FCI paper's simulation at 20 measured variables with up to 8
  hidden, run at the paper's settings and at Andrey's defaults, and at 100 with 10, run at the
  paper's settings. It is 1.05 to 5.8 times faster at 20 variables and 1.85 times faster at 100.
- GRaSP-FCI ties Tetrad 7.6.10's GRaSP-FCI in 72 of 73 comparisons on the same data and is better
  in one. It is 1.16 to 5.8 times faster at 20 variables and 7.6 times faster at 100.

Tetrad 7.6.3, the authors' earlier release, removes edges by the GFCI paper's triangle rule. At the
paper's settings it made fewer errors at mean degree 4 and 6 and at 100 variables; at Andrey's
defaults at mean degree 2 it made more. Andrey and Tetrad 7.6.10 test every edge by default, and
`edge_removal="triangles"` runs the triangle rule.

The [release card](../docs/benchmarks/0.15.0.md) has every comparison and how we measured them.

## Limits

- Each method takes one environment variable, so a design with several context variables runs with
  them merged into one label. The card reports FCI-JCI on such designs outside its claim, where it
  tied pcalg too.
- With the default Fisher z test or BIC score, `env` is read as a number, and text labels as their
  codes 0, 1, ... in sorted order. With three or more environments, a shift in one of them alone
  can go unnoticed, and recoding the labels can change the result. `indep_test="cond_gauss"`, or
  `score_func="cond_gauss"` with `categorical=[]`, reads each label as a category.
- When every variable changes across the environments, as on Sachs's conditions, the index directs
  no edge.
- CD-NOD and the score and permutation searches assume no hidden common cause beyond the
  environment. When one is plausible, use FCI-JCI, GFCI, BOSS-FCI, or GRaSP-FCI.
- With `env`, give hill climbing `n_starts` above 1. One climb can direct a chain against the index
  and list every variable on it as changing.
- `env` in GES, hill climbing, Tabu search, GFCI, BOSS, GRaSP, BOSS-FCI, and GRaSP-FCI is
  experimental: no benchmark has measured it yet.
- BOSS-FCI and GRaSP-FCI recover only part of the PAG on their paper's simulation, and so does
  Tetrad: hidden variables leave many pairs joined only through them. At mean degree 6, their
  errors were about three quarters of the empty graph's.

# Ready for real data

In 0.9.0, the rocket lifted off. In 0.10.0, we charted how far it can go.

Now comes chapter three: landing on real data.

Real tables are messy. Values go missing. Columns come in different units. Some columns are
categories, not numbers. You need to know which edges to trust, and you already know things the
data cannot tell you.

In 0.11.0, Andrey handles each of these. We take one real table, 5,569 adults from a national
health survey, from raw columns to a causal graph, in one short script.

## TL;DR

- **Gaps:** PC, FCI, and the other constraint-based methods read, for each test, the samples it
  can use, so they keep the third of the adults that dropping every incomplete one would lose.
- **Units:** the same graph whether blood pressure is in mmHg or kPa.
- **Mixed types:** declare a column as a category, and the default test becomes one for mixed data.
- **What you already know:** background knowledge in every family, 27 of 34 methods. On the survey,
  it removes all 10 arrows that pointed into age or sex.
- **How sure each edge is:** `andrey.bootstrap` refits on bootstrap samples and gives each edge's
  frequency and an averaged graph.
- **New defaults:** the extended BIC for ten score searches, which cuts GES's errors on 200
  variables from 211 to 24, and standardized simulated data.

## A real table

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) measures the health of people across
the United States. Its 2017-2018 cycle is public domain, published by the US Centers for Disease
Control and Prevention. We join six of its files on each participant's number and keep the adults,
aged 20 and over, with eleven columns:

| Column | What it measures | Missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `age` | age in years | 0% |
| `sex` | male or female | 0% |
| `income` | family income as a ratio to the poverty line | 14% |
| `bmi` | body mass index, kg/m² | 7% |
| `waist` | waist circumference, cm | 11% |
| `systolic`, `diastolic` | blood pressure, mmHg | 16% |
| `pulse` | resting pulse, beats per minute | 10% |
| `hba1c` | glycated hemoglobin, % | 10% |
| `cholesterol`, `hdl` | total and HDL cholesterol, mg/dL | 11% |

Not everyone came to the exam, had their blood pressure read, or gave blood, and some did not report
their income. Only 3,694 of the 5,569 adults have every value. We do not use the survey weights, so
the graph describes this sample, not the US population.

## Gaps

The usual way to handle a gap is to drop every sample that has one, listwise deletion. Here that
drops 1,875 adults, a third of the table, each missing at least one value.

PC, FCI, CD-NOD, IAMB, Inter-IAMB, MMPC, PCMCI, PCMCI+, and LPCMCI take the table as it is, with
`NaN` where a value is missing. Each conditional-independence test reads the samples where all of its
variables are observed, test-wise deletion. A test of age and HbA1c given waist keeps an adult whose blood pressure is
missing, because it does not need it.

On the survey, the two ways agree on most of the graph. Both find 25 edges, and they differ in 6
places, only 2 of which are about whether an edge is there at all: sex and waist are adjacent only
on the complete adults, and sex and cholesterol only on every adult. The other 4 are directions. Where
the two disagree, the bootstrap below shows which edges hold up.

## Units

Blood pressure in mmHg or kPa, cholesterol in mg/dL or mmol/L: a unit is a choice of scale, and it
should not change the causes. The script converts four columns to SI units and fits again. The
graph is the same, with 0 differences.

## Mixed types

Sex is a category, not a number. In pandas, give it the `category` type, and Andrey reads it as
one:

```python
data["sex"] = data["sex"].map({1: "male", 2: "female"}).astype("category")
```

With no test named, PC now picks it from the column types you declare: `gsq` when every column is
a category, the conditional Gaussian test `cond_gauss` when some are, and Fisher's z when none are.
Here it picks `cond_gauss`, and the fit records the test it used. Missing values work with it too.

A column of small whole numbers, such as 1 and 2 for sex, may also be a category. Andrey does not
guess: it warns and reads the column as numbers until you declare it.

## What you already know

Nothing in a body causes a person's age or sex. The data do not know that, and PC, left to itself,
draws 10 arrows into age or sex, among them pulse into age and HDL cholesterol into sex.

Tell it with `andrey.Knowledge`: age and sex in the first tier, every other column after them, so
no edge may point back into the first tier.

```python
known = andrey.Knowledge(tiers=[["age", "sex"], [c for c in data if c not in ("age", "sex")]])
```

With it, 0 arrows point into age or sex. Knowledge also takes required, forbidden, and allowed
edges, and 27 of the 34 methods accept it, in every family.

## How sure is each edge

One fit gives one graph. It does not say which edges would come back with a different sample of
adults. `andrey.bootstrap` refits on 100 bootstrap samples, each drawn with replacement, and
counts how often each edge appears. With `andrey.config(num_workers=4)`, the fits run four at a time.

Of the edges in at least half the fits, eight appear in all of them: age into systolic pressure,
pulse, and HbA1c, and sex into BMI, blood pressure, pulse, and HDL cholesterol. Then come age into
HDL cholesterol (0.95) and waist into HDL cholesterol (0.86). Further down, near one half, are the
edges a single fit may or may not draw, such as pulse and diastolic pressure (0.50).

## Hand it on

The bootstrap also returns an averaged graph: the pairs adjacent in at least half the fits, each
with its most frequent edge. Like every Andrey graph, it converts to NetworkX, a pcalg matrix,
NumPy, or Graphviz DOT text, which DoWhy reads to estimate an effect. A CPDAG turns into one of its
DAGs with `to_dag()`.

## New defaults

**The extended BIC.** GES, GIES, XGES, hill climbing, Tabu search, BOSS, GRaSP, exact search,
MMHC, and RSMAX2 score continuous data with the extended BIC
([Chen and Chen, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/asn034)). Its penalty grows with the number
of variables, so a large graph keeps fewer false edges. On 200 variables and 2,000 samples, GES's
errors fall from 211 to 24. GFCI keeps the standard BIC. Pass `ebic_gamma=0.0` for the graphs of
before.

**Standardized simulated data.** `andrey.data.sample_scm` and `andrey.data.generate` give every
column a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1, so no method can read the causal order off the
variances a simulation happens to give. Pass `scale="raw"` for the values of before.

**One name for each argument.** The level of a test is `alpha` in every method, the BIC's weight is
`penalty_discount`, the largest lag is `tau_max`, and the random seed is `seed`. The former names
still work in this release, with a warning that gives the new one. The
[upgrade guide](../docs/guides/upgrading.md) shows the common calls before and after, and the
[changelog](../docs/changelog.md) lists every renamed argument, changed default, and changed
output.

## Limits

- **Missing values.** Test-wise deletion is sound when values are missing completely at random,
  and FCI stays sound when no missingness mechanism causally affects another. When whether a value
  is missing depends on the variables, either deletion can bias a test.
- **Mixed data.** The conditional Gaussian and degenerate Gaussian tests are experimental.
- **The bootstrap.** A frequency says how stable an edge is across samples of the same adults, not
  the probability that it is true. A method that is wrong in the same way on every sample is wrong
  with a frequency of 1.
- **One survey.** The graph comes from one survey cycle, without its weights, and from the eleven
  columns we chose. A column left out can be a cause of two that are in.

# Permutation invariance

Give two people the same table, one with its columns in a different order, and ask each to learn
a causal graph from it. They should get the same graph. Column order carries no information: it
comes from a database export, a DataFrame, or whoever wrote the CSV. A method whose graph does not
depend on that order is permutation invariant.

Strictly, such a method is permutation equivariant: reorder the columns, and the graph's nodes
reorder the same way. In this post, "the same graph" means the same graph up to that relabeling.

Many causal-discovery methods are not invariant. When two steps look equally good, they take the
first one they find, and "first" means the leftmost column.
[Colombo and Maathuis (2014)](https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/colombo14a.html) showed this for PC.
It matters for three reasons:

- **Reproducibility.** Two analysts with the same data get different graphs.
- **Trust.** An edge that flips when you reorder the columns was never supported by the data.
- **Benchmarks.** Data generators often write columns in an order tied to the graph, so a method
  that favors the first column gains or loses accuracy it did not earn.

We found order dependence in six of our methods and in our own benchmark. This post reports how we
found it, why it happens, how we removed it, and how Andrey compares with other packages.

## TL;DR

- In 0.7.0, PC, hill climbing, tabu search, BOSS, GRaSP, and GIN are order-independent, in the
  sense of Colombo and Maathuis: column order no longer decides their graph.
- PC: reordering the columns no longer changes PC's graph in 14 of the 16 settings we measured. It
  used to change the graph in 40% to 95% of reorderings. PC also makes fewer errors on average in
  all 16, clearly so in 11.
- Hill climbing and tabu search: reordering the columns changes the graph on none of 150
  categorical datasets, against 112 to 147 of 150 in 0.6.0, depending on the method and score,
  with the same accuracy.
- BOSS and GRaSP: reordering the columns changes the graph on none of 150 linear-Gaussian
  datasets, against 146 and 149 of 150 in 0.6.0.
- GIN: ten reorderings of the columns change the latent order on none of 15 datasets with 2 to 4
  hidden causes, against 11 of 15 when its root test used each cluster's first column.
- Our benchmark data now come with shuffled columns. Column order can no longer help or hurt a
  method in our own measurements.

## How we found it

It started with hill climbing. On the 0.6.0 categorical benchmark, pgmpy's hill climbing had fewer
errors than Andrey's on 17 datasets. We looked for a bug in a score and found a tie instead. Under
BDeu and BIC, adding `x -> y` and adding `y -> x` can score exactly the same, and Andrey gave the
tie to the column that came first. That tie decided every one of the 17. With the columns reversed,
Andrey had no more errors than pgmpy on 14 of them.
[Kitson and Constantinou (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08952) found that variable order moves
hill climbing's graphs more than many other choices do.

Then we looked at our own data. Our data generators wrote columns in an order tied to the graph:
on a scale-free graph, the biggest hubs came first. A method that breaks ties by column order then
gains or loses accuracy because of that order, not the data.
[Reisach, Seiler, and Weichwald (2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.13647) found a related artifact
in simulated data: variance that grows along the causal order. Ours was simpler: the column order
itself followed the graph.

This misled us once. With columns in generated order, the new PC rule looked worse than the old one
on scale-free graphs, by 1.0 and 4.8 endpoint errors on average at the two significance levels. With
the same columns in random order, it was better, by 1.0 and 3.1. Below, each node of a scale-free
graph carries its column number, as our generators wrote it and as they shuffle it now, beside that
reversal.

## Why it happens

A method depends on column order when it chooses between two equally good steps by taking the first
one it finds, or when it starts from the columns' positions. PC's case fits on five variables.

We generated 300 rows in which A causes B, C, and D, and each of them causes E. PC orients a
collider `x -> y <- z` when `x` and `z` look independent and `y` did not separate them. Here, B, C,
and D look independent given A, so PC proposes three colliders at E, the true ones. But A and E also
look independent given B, with a p-value of 0.052, just above the significance level of 0.05. So
PC also proposes `A -> C <- E` and `A -> D <- E`, which point the edges between E and C and between
E and D the wrong way.

Two colliders that disagree about an edge cannot both win. The first one PC orients claims the edge.
The column-order rule visits colliders by their middle column, left to right. Whenever C or D sits
to the left of E, a weak collider claims its edge first. Across the 120 orders of the columns, the
rule returns 4 different graphs, and the true one on only 40. The p-value rule visits the colliders
whose pair was most clearly separated first, so E's colliders always win.

We found the same pattern in five places:

- **PC.** When two colliders disagree about one edge, PC kept whichever came first in column order.
  On a seeded grid, the output changed on 116 of 150 reorderings.
- **Hill climbing and tabu search.** An exact score tie went to the column that came first.
- **BOSS and GRaSP.** Both search over orders of the variables. BOSS started from the column order
  and visited variables in a seeded shuffle of their positions; GRaSP shuffled the positions for its
  starting order and its lists of parents. With the same seed, a reordering changed BOSS's graph on
  146 of 150 linear-Gaussian datasets and GRaSP's on 149.
- **GIN.** The root test, which decides which hidden cause comes first, built each cluster's
  stand-in from the cluster's first column.
- **FCI.** The possible-d-separation step visits nodes in column order. On graphs with 50 to 200
  variables, reordering the columns changed FCI's result on 51% of the reorderings we tried.

## How we fixed it

One idea runs through every fix: replace a column's position with something computed from its
data, so the choice moves with the column.

### PC

PC orients conflicting colliders by the p-value that separated each collider's pair, highest first.
We adapted this rule from [Ramsey (2016)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.00378);
[Colombo and Maathuis (2014)](https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/colombo14a.html) give the background.
The collider step no longer depends on column order. `collider_order="column"` keeps the old rule.

We compared the two rules on shuffled data across 16 settings and 1,040 datasets. These cover
linear-Gaussian data on Erdos-Renyi and scale-free graphs with 10 to 200 variables, Student-t noise,
and the categorical and mixed data settings of the 0.6.0 release card.

- **Order.** With the p-value rule, no reordering changed PC's graph in 14 of the 16 settings; in
  the other two, 5% and 10% of reorderings did. With the column-order rule, 40% to 95% did.
- **Errors.** The p-value rule has fewer endpoint errors on average in all 16 settings. The
  difference is clear in 11; in the four categorical settings and one mixed setting, its 95%
  interval includes zero.
- **Time.** The p-value rule takes at most about 3% longer.

### Hill climbing and tabu search

Hill climbing and tabu search now break exact score ties by the signs of each column's changes
from row to row, or, for a categorical column, by which rows share a category, instead of by the
column's position. That rank moves with the column, and small rounding differences between
machines do not change it. We fit both methods on 150 categorical datasets with 10, 20, and 40
variables, each in its own column order and five reorderings.

| Method | Score | Datasets where a reordering changed the graph, 0.6.0 | 0.7.0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hill climbing | BDeu | 145 of 150 | 0 of 150 |
| Hill climbing | BIC | 147 of 150 | 0 of 150 |
| Tabu search | BDeu | 112 of 150 | 0 of 150 |
| Tabu search | BIC | 118 of 150 | 0 of 150 |

Accuracy is the same: for each method and score, the change in mean errors from 0.6.0 is between
-0.06 and -0.26, and every 95% interval for it includes zero. Hill climbing's median is 9.0 errors
in both releases.

### BOSS and GRaSP

BOSS and GRaSP now search the columns sorted by the same rank, so their starting order, their
seeded shuffles, and their score ties follow each column's data instead of its position. Different
seeds still give different graphs, and a seed's graph can differ from 0.6.0's. We fit both methods
in Andrey and in causal-learn on 150 linear-Gaussian datasets on Erdos-Renyi graphs with 10, 20,
and 50 variables, each in its own column order and five reorderings.

| Method | Datasets where a reordering changed the graph: causal-learn | Andrey 0.6.0 | Andrey 0.7.0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| BOSS | 137 of 150 | 146 of 150 | 0 of 150 |
| GRaSP | 150 of 150 | 149 of 150 | 0 of 150 |

We compare accuracy with causal-learn's BOSS and GRaSP on the same datasets and column orders, at
the same penalty, rather than with 0.6.0, whose errors partly came from the column order. Andrey's
errors are indistinguishable from causal-learn's: the paired difference from causal-learn's mean
over the six orders is +0.01 errors for BOSS (95% interval -0.99 to +1.01) and +0.84 for GRaSP
(-0.78 to +2.46).

### GIN

GIN's root test now runs once per member of the clusters it compares and pools the p-values, and
the search visits the columns sorted by the same rank. On the 15 benchmark datasets with 2 to 4
hidden causes, ten reorderings of the columns change the latent order on none, against 11 of 15
with the first-column test. The clusters never changed.

### FCI

`fci` and `gfci` accept `possible_dsep="order_free"`. This option builds each possible-d-separation
set after the search instead of while it runs. On graphs with 50 to 200 variables, it lowers the
share of reorderings that change FCI's result from 51% to 43%. Endpoint errors change by less than
half a percent. That step can run up to 4.53 times as many tests. The option is off by default.

Simulated data can understate the problem. We also ran both versions on six real tables, each under
200 random column orders (20 for the full superconductivity table). We counted the orders that
changed the result and the different graphs, including the graph from the original order.

| Dataset | Variables | Rows | Orders that change the result | Different graphs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sachs protein signaling | 11 | 853 | 0 / 0 of 200 | 1 / 1 |
| Wine | 13 | 178 | 0 / 0 of 200 | 1 / 1 |
| Diabetes | 10 | 442 | 148 / 111 of 200 | 7 / 4 |
| Breast cancer | 30 | 569 | 195 / 195 of 200 | 30 / 30 |
| Superconductivity, 1,000-row sample | 81 | 1,000 | 185 / 191 of 200 | 48 / 46 |
| Superconductivity | 81 | 21,263 | 20 / 20 of 20 | 21 / 21 |

Each cell gives the default first and `possible_dsep="order_free"` second. We used the observational
measurements of Sachs et al. (2005); the wine, diabetes, and breast cancer tables bundled with
scikit-learn; and the UCI superconductivity table (Hamidieh, 2018).

On Sachs and wine, column order never mattered. It mattered a lot on the other four tables. On the
full superconductivity table, every reordering gave a different graph. The order-free sets helped on
diabetes, did slightly worse on the superconductivity sample, and made no difference on the rest. On
the full superconductivity table, they ran half again as many independence tests (14,513 against
9,628). The whole fit took about the same time, 5.0 s.

### The benchmark data

The data generators now shuffle the columns along with the true graph (`andrey.data.sample_scm`,
`sample_discrete`, and `sample_mixed`; `shuffle=False` keeps the drawn order). So does
`andrey.data.load_dataset` for the bnlearn networks, whose files list their variables close to
causal order, and so do the inputs of our recorded test outputs and the data of the example
notebooks. Every benchmark from 0.7.0 uses these generators. The summaries we published earlier
keep their numbers.

## How Andrey compares

COMPARISON

Agreement with another package is a measurement, not the goal. On the seven settings where we also
ran causal-learn, which keeps the column-order rule, the new PC rule returns causal-learn's graph on
7% to 60% of datasets, against 75% to 100% with the old rule, and it makes fewer errors.

## Where order still enters

- **PC's orientation rules.** After the collider step, Meek's rules can still depend on order,
  though rarely: 5% and 10% of reorderings changed PC's graph on Erdos-Renyi graphs with mean
  degree 4.
- **FCI on sampled data.** `possible_dsep="order_free"` removes the order from that step only when
  the tests are exact. On sampled data, FCI's result can still depend on column order.

For the same graph on every machine and every run, sort your columns by name before fitting.

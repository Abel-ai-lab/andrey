# Column order

Reorder your data columns, and you should get the same graph. Column order carries no information.
It comes from the order a CSV export or a DataFrame happened to use. A method that depends on it
gives two people with the same data two different answers. In 0.7.0, PC, hill climbing, and tabu
search no longer let column order decide their graph.

## TL;DR

- PC: reordering the columns no longer changes PC's graph in 14 of the 16 settings we measured. It
  used to change the graph in 40% to 95% of reorderings. PC also makes fewer errors on average in
  all 16, clearly so in 11.
- Hill climbing and tabu search: reordering the columns changes the graph on none of 30 categorical
  datasets, down from 22 to 29 in 0.6.0.
- Our benchmark data now come with shuffled columns. Column order can no longer help or hurt a
  method in our own measurements.

We show the problem with a small example, explain how column order affected each method and our
benchmark, and report what changed.

## Where column order entered

A method depends on column order when it chooses between two equally good steps by taking the first
one it finds. We found three places where this happens.

- **FCI.** The possible-d-separation step visits nodes in column order. On graphs with 50 to 200
  variables, reordering the columns changed FCI's result on 51% of the reorderings we tried.
- **PC.** When two colliders disagree about one edge, PC kept whichever came first in column order.
  On a seeded grid, the output changed on 116 of 150 reorderings.
- **Hill climbing.** Under BDeu and BIC, adding `x -> y` and adding `y -> x` can score exactly the
  same. The tie went to the column that came first. On the 0.6.0 categorical benchmark, this tie
  decided every dataset where pgmpy's hill climbing had fewer errors than Andrey's. With the columns
  reversed, Andrey had no more errors than pgmpy on 14 of those 17.

## The bias in our own benchmark

Our data generators wrote columns in an order tied to the graph. On a scale-free graph, the biggest
hubs came first. A method that breaks ties by column order then gains or loses accuracy because of
that order, not the data.

This misled us once. With columns in generated order, the new PC rule looked worse than the old one
on scale-free graphs, by 1.0 and 4.8 endpoint errors on average at the two significance levels. With
the same columns in random order, it was better, by 1.0 and 3.1.

Starting in 0.7.0, the data generators shuffle the columns along with the true graph
(`andrey.data.sample_scm`, `sample_discrete`, and `sample_mixed`; `shuffle=False` keeps the drawn
order). Every benchmark from 0.7.0 uses these generators. The summaries we published earlier keep
their numbers.

## What changed in 0.7.0

### PC

PC orients conflicting colliders by the p-value that separated each collider's pair, highest first.
We adapted this rule from [Ramsey (2016)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.00378);
[Colombo and Maathuis (2014)](https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/colombo14a.html) give the background.
The collider step no longer depends on column order. `collider_order="column"` keeps the old rule.

We compared the two rules on shuffled data across 16 settings and 1,040 datasets. These cover
linear-Gaussian data on Erdos-Renyi and scale-free graphs with 10 to 200 variables, Student-t noise,
and the categorical and mixed data settings of the 0.6.0 release card.

- **Order.** With the p-value rule, no reordering changed PC's graph in 14 of the 16 settings; in
  the other two, 5% and 10% of reorderings did. With the column-order rule, 40% to 95% did.
- **Errors.** The p-value rule has fewer endpoint errors on average in all 16 settings. The
  difference is clear in 11; in the four categorical settings and one mixed setting, its 95%
  interval includes zero.
- **Time.** The p-value rule takes at most about 3% longer.

### Hill climbing and tabu search

Hill climbing and tabu search now break exact score ties with a hash of each column's values. The
hash moves with the column. We fit both methods on 30 categorical datasets with 10, 20, and 40
variables, five column orders each.

| Method | Score | Datasets where a reordering changed the graph, 0.6.0 | 0.7.0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hill climbing | BDeu | 28 of 30 | 0 of 30 |
| Hill climbing | BIC | 29 of 30 | 0 of 30 |
| Tabu search | BDeu | 22 of 30 | 0 of 30 |
| Tabu search | BIC | 24 of 30 | 0 of 30 |

Accuracy is about the same. For hill climbing, the median error is 9.0 with BDeu and 8.5 with BIC in
both releases. For tabu search, it is 5.5, down from 6.0.

### FCI

`fci` and `gfci` accept `possible_dsep="order_free"`. This option builds each possible-d-separation
set after the search instead of while it runs. On graphs with 50 to 200 variables, it lowers the
share of reorderings that change FCI's result from 51% to 43%. Endpoint errors change by less than
half a percent. That step can run up to 4.53 times as many tests. The option is off by default.

Simulated data can understate the problem. We also ran both versions on six real tables, each under
200 random column orders (20 for the full superconductivity table). We counted the orders that
changed the result and the different graphs, including the graph from the original order.

| Dataset | Variables | Rows | Orders that change the result | Different graphs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sachs protein signaling | 11 | 853 | 0 / 0 of 200 | 1 / 1 |
| Wine | 13 | 178 | 0 / 0 of 200 | 1 / 1 |
| Diabetes | 10 | 442 | 148 / 111 of 200 | 7 / 4 |
| Breast cancer | 30 | 569 | 195 / 195 of 200 | 30 / 30 |
| Superconductivity, 1,000-row sample | 81 | 1,000 | 185 / 191 of 200 | 48 / 46 |
| Superconductivity | 81 | 21,263 | 20 / 20 of 20 | 21 / 21 |

Each cell gives the default first and `possible_dsep="order_free"` second. We used the observational
measurements of Sachs et al. (2005); the wine, diabetes, and breast cancer tables bundled with
scikit-learn; and the UCI superconductivity table (Hamidieh, 2018).

On Sachs and wine, column order never mattered. It mattered a lot on the other four tables. On the
full superconductivity table, every reordering gave a different graph. The order-free sets helped on
diabetes, did slightly worse on the superconductivity sample, and made no difference on the rest. On
the full superconductivity table, they ran half again as many independence tests (14,513 against
9,628). The whole fit took about the same time, 5.0 s.

## Where order still enters

- **PC's orientation rules.** After the collider step, Meek's rules can still depend on order,
  though rarely: 5% and 10% of reorderings changed PC's graph on Erdos-Renyi graphs with mean
  degree 4.
- **FCI on sampled data.** `possible_dsep="order_free"` removes the order from that step only when
  the tests are exact. On sampled data, FCI's result can still depend on column order.

For the same graph on every machine and every run, sort your columns by name before fitting.

## What we learned

A benchmark can hide a bias that helps or hurts a method. We therefore shuffle columns in the data
generator, not in the method. Agreement with another package is a measurement, not the goal. On the
seven settings where we also ran causal-learn, which keeps the column-order rule, the new PC rule
returns causal-learn's graph on 7% to 60% of datasets, against 75% to 100% with the old rule, and it
makes fewer errors.

## Other highlights

- **Seven more methods supported.** GIES, exact search, CD-NOD, MultiGroupDirectLiNGAM, GFCI,
  GIN, and CALM are supported, each measured against a package that implements it. GIN runs more
  than 20x faster than Tetrad. CALM runs more than 15x faster than causal-learn, and CD-NOD more
  than 15x faster from 20 variables, both with fewer errors.
- **Faster GES.** GES runs more than 60x faster than causal-learn at 50 to 150 variables, and more
  than 300x at 150, with the same graph on every dataset.

- **CAM-UV removed.** On simulated nonlinear data with hidden common causes, its graphs had more
  errors than the empty graph at every size we measured.

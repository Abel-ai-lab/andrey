# Ready for real data

## TL;DR

- **Gaps:** PC, FCI, and the other constraint-based methods read, for each test, the samples it
  can use, so they keep the third of the adults that dropping every incomplete one would lose.
- **Why values go missing:** new in 0.16.0, MVPC corrects PC when whether a value is missing
  depends on other variables, EM fits the score searches, NOTEARS, DAGMA, and CALM on incomplete
  data, and `andrey.data.missingness` simulates missing values whose cause you know.
- **Units:** the same graph whether blood pressure is in mmHg or kPa.
- **Mixed types:** declare a column as a category, and the default test becomes one for mixed data.
- **What you already know:** background knowledge in 42 of 47 methods, every family but pairwise.
  On the survey, it removes all 10 arrows that pointed into age or sex. New in 0.16.0, it can
  also state causal paths and hidden common causes.
- **How sure each edge is:** `andrey.bootstrap` refits on resamples of the rows and gives each
  edge's frequency and an averaged graph.
- **Defaults:** the extended BIC for the score searches, which in the 0.11.0 benchmarks cut GES's
  median errors from 87 to 25, and standardized simulated data.

---

Andrey is ready for real-world chaos. We built the basics in 0.11.0, and 0.16.0 adds more ways to
handle missing values and to state what you already know.

Real tables are messy. Values go missing. Columns come in different units. Some columns are
categories, not numbers. You need to know which edges to trust, and you already know things the
data cannot tell you.

Andrey handles each of these. We take one real table, 5,569 adults from a national health
survey, from raw columns to a causal graph, in one short script.

## Gaps

The usual way to handle a gap is to drop every sample that has one, listwise deletion. Here that
drops 1,875 adults, a third of the table, each missing at least one value.

PC, FCI, CD-NOD, FCI-JCI, IAMB, Inter-IAMB, MMPC, PCMCI, PCMCI+, and LPCMCI take the table as it
is, with `NaN` where a value is missing. Each conditional-independence test reads the samples where
all of its variables are observed, test-wise deletion, which is their default, `missing="testwise"`.
A test of age and HbA1c given waist keeps an adult whose blood pressure is missing, because it does
not need it. ANM, PNL, and LOCI read the rows where both columns of their pair are observed.

On the survey, the two ways agree on most of the graph. Both find 25 edges, and they differ in 6
places, only 2 of which are about whether an edge is there at all: sex and waist are adjacent only
on the complete adults, and sex and cholesterol only on every adult. The other 4 are directions.
Where the two disagree, the bootstrap below shows which edges hold up.

On wider tables, listwise deletion runs out of data. In the 0.11.0 benchmarks, on simulations of
20 variables and 1,000 samples with values missing completely at random, dropping the incomplete
samples left about 125 when a tenth of the values were missing, about 10 when a fifth were, and
almost none when 30% were. Test-wise deletion kept every sample and was never significantly worse
than listwise deletion. PC's errors rose only from 4.0 on the complete data to 6.5 with a tenth
missing and 7.5 with 30%. The [0.11.0 release card](../docs/benchmarks/0.11.0.md) gives every size,
rate, and test.

## Why values go missing

Test-wise deletion, like dropping incomplete samples, works when whether a value is missing is
unrelated to the values themselves: missing completely at random. In the survey, that means
people skipped the blood test for reasons unrelated to their health. If people with high blood
sugar skipped it more often, the samples a test reads would no longer be a fair sample of the
adults. The test could then find a dependence that is not there or miss one that is. 0.16.0 lets
you simulate such data and gives you two methods that correct for it.

**Simulate it.** `andrey.data.missingness` draws missing values whose cause you know, so you can see
how a method fares before you trust it on your own data. `mcar` makes values missing completely at
random, `mar` makes them depend on columns that are always observed, `mnar` on other columns that
can go missing, and `self_masking` on the value itself. Each follows a logistic form (Muzellec et
al., 2020) or the quantile form of the MVPC simulations, and `parents=` lets you specify the causes
of each column's missingness yourself. Pass one to `sample_scm` or `SCM.sample`, which draw the mask
from the seed's own stream. With the same seed, omitting it gives you the complete data. You can
also mask any complete table with `missingness.draw`. The dataset's `missing` records the mask
and the causes of each column's missingness:

```python
import andrey
from andrey.data import missingness, sample_scm

ds = sample_scm(d=10, n=2000, seed=0, density=2, missing=missingness.mar(0.2))
ds.missing.parents  # the observed columns that each column's missingness depends on
```

**Correct PC with MVPC.** `andrey.pc(data, missing="mvpc")` runs Missing-Value PC
([Tu et al., 2019](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v89/tu19a.html)). After finding the test-wise
skeleton, it finds the causes of each column's missingness. It then retests the edges that
missingness could have kept, using virtual data that correct for those causes. It corrects
Fisher's z and Student's t, so it does not run on the survey, where sex is a category.

**Fit the score searches with EM.** GES, XGES, hill climbing, Tabu search, exact search, BOSS, and
GRaSP with the BIC, and NOTEARS, DAGMA, and CALM, read the data through its covariance, so they
cannot delete rows per test. `missing="em"` fits them by structural EM (Friedman, 1997;
[Gao et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13869)) in 10 rounds. In each round, under a
linear-Gaussian model of the current graph, it computes the expected covariance of the complete
data, runs the search on it, and fits the model again to the graph found.

```python
andrey.pc(ds.data, missing="mvpc").metadata["missingness_parents"]
andrey.ges(ds.data, missing="em").metadata["em"]["log_likelihood"]  # after each round
```

Every method that accepts missing values names its handling with the same argument, `missing`. The
default, `None`, keeps each method's own handling, and `metadata["missing"]` records the handling a
fit used.

## Units

Blood pressure in mmHg or kPa, cholesterol in mg/dL or mmol/L: a unit is a choice of scale, and it
should not change the causes. The script converts four columns to SI units and fits again. The
graph is the same, with 0 differences.

## Mixed types

Sex is a category, not a number. In pandas, give it the `category` type, and Andrey reads it as
one:

```python
data["sex"] = data["sex"].map({1: "male", 2: "female"}).astype("category")
```

With no test named, PC picks it from the column types you declare: `gsq` when every column is
a category, the conditional Gaussian test `cond_gauss` when some are, and Fisher's z when none are.
Here it picks `cond_gauss`, and the fit records the test it used. Missing values work with it too.

A column of small whole numbers, such as 1 and 2 for sex, may also be a category. Andrey does not
guess: it warns and reads the column as numbers until you declare it.

## What you already know

Nothing in a body causes a person's age or sex. The data do not tell PC that. Without background
knowledge, PC draws 10 arrows into age or sex, among them pulse into age and HDL cholesterol into
sex.

State this with `andrey.Knowledge`: put age and sex in the first tier and every other column after
them, so no edge may point back into the first tier.

```python
known = andrey.Knowledge(tiers=[["age", "sex"], [c for c in data if c not in ("age", "sex")]])
```

With it, 0 arrows point into age or sex. Knowledge also takes required, forbidden, and allowed
edges, and 42 of the 47 methods accept it, in every family but the pairwise one.

New in 0.16.0, knowledge can also state what you know about causal paths and hidden causes.
`required_paths` lists the pairs `(a, b)` in which `a` causes `b`, directly or through other
variables; `forbidden_paths`, the pairs in which it does not; and `confounded`, the pairs that
share a cause the table lacks. Suppose you know that BMI and waist size do not cause each
other but share a cause the survey did not measure, body fat. FCI, which allows for hidden causes,
takes that as:

```python
known = andrey.Knowledge(
    confounded=[("bmi", "waist")],  # a hidden common cause: body fat
    forbidden_paths=[("bmi", "waist"), ("waist", "bmi")],  # neither causes the other
)
pag = andrey.fci(data, knowledge=known)
```

No test removes a `confounded` pair. On a pair the tests keep, a forbidden path puts an arrowhead at
its cause, so the two claims together make `bmi <-> waist`. FCI and the other PAG methods honor all
three parts. PC, GES, BOSS, and the other methods that return a CPDAG honor the two path parts: of
the DAGs their graph stands for, they keep those that meet every claim, and list in
`metadata["ancestor_clauses"]` the claims no directed edge can show. DirectLiNGAM reads the path
parts to narrow its causal order. A method given a part it does not honor raises an error that names
the methods that do. The parts are supported in PC, GES, BOSS, FCI, GFCI, BOSS-FCI, LPCMCI, and
DirectLiNGAM, where true claims measured no worse than the same fit without them and than reference
packages given the same knowledge, and experimental in the other methods, which we have not
measured.

## How sure is each edge

One fit gives one graph. It does not say which edges would come back with a different sample of
adults. `andrey.bootstrap` refits on 100 resamples and counts how often each edge appears. Each
resample is half of the adults, drawn without replacement: a resample drawn with replacement repeats
rows, so a test would see more evidence than the resample holds. With `andrey.config(n_jobs=4)`, the
fits run four at a time.

Of the edges in at least half the fits, eight appear in all of them: age into systolic pressure,
pulse, and HbA1c, and sex into BMI, blood pressure, pulse, and HDL cholesterol. Then come age into
HDL cholesterol (0.83), and waist and total cholesterol into HDL cholesterol (0.77 each). Further
down, near one half, are the edges a single fit may or may not draw, such as diastolic into systolic
pressure (0.52).

Read the frequencies as a ranking. In the 0.11.0 simulations, which drew each resample with
replacement, of the edges seen in more than nine fits in ten, 98% to 100% were true, and of those
seen in fewer than one in ten, about 1% or fewer. The frequencies in between order the edges from
likely to unlikely, but are not probabilities.

## Hand it on

The bootstrap also returns an averaged graph: each pair takes the state it has most often, no edge
included, and the result is a valid graph of the method's kind. Like every Andrey graph, it
converts to NetworkX, a pcalg matrix, NumPy, or Graphviz DOT text, which DoWhy reads to estimate an
effect. A CPDAG turns into one of its DAGs with `to_dag()`.

## Defaults

**The extended BIC.** GES, GIES, GnIES, XGES, hill climbing, Tabu search, BOSS, GRaSP, exact
search, MMHC, and RSMAX2 score continuous data with the extended BIC
([Chen and Chen, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/asn034)). Its penalty grows with the number
of variables, so a large graph keeps fewer false edges. In the 0.11.0 benchmarks, over 299
simulated datasets, GES's median errors fell from 87 to 25, and BOSS's from 69 to 15. GFCI,
BOSS-FCI, and GRaSP-FCI keep the standard BIC. Pass `ebic_gamma=0.0` for the standard BIC.

**Standardized simulated data.** `andrey.data.sample_scm` and `SCM.sample` give every column a mean
of 0 and a standard deviation of 1, so no method can read the causal order off the variances a
simulation happens to give. Pass `scale="raw"` for the values as drawn.

**One name for each argument.** The level of a test is `alpha` in every method, the BIC's weight is
`penalty_discount`, the largest lag is `tau_max`, and the random seed is `seed`. A former name
raises a `TypeError` that gives the current one and the release that renamed it. The
[upgrade guide](../docs/guides/upgrading.md) shows the common calls before and after, and the
[changelog](../docs/changelog.md) lists every renamed argument, changed default, and changed
output.

## Limits

**Why values are missing matters.** Test-wise deletion is sound when values are missing completely
at random, and FCI tolerates more than PC; the
[getting-started guide](../docs/guides/getting-started.md#missing-values) explains when. EM is also
sound when values are missing at random given the observed ones. It has no guarantee when whether
a value is missing depends on the value itself. MVPC's correction holds when no column's
missingness causes a variable, every independence in the observed rows also holds in the missing
ones, no column's missingness is a deterministic function of others, and no value causes its own
missingness. It also needs linear-Gaussian variables and the two conditions of its paper: it can
keep an extra edge when the missingness of a variable it conditions on is a common effect of the
pair it tests. You cannot check these assumptions from the data.

**MVPC and EM are measured on simulated data.** MVPC is supported, as is EM in NOTEARS, hill
climbing, and Tabu search. Against the MVPC authors' code, MissDAG, and bnlearn's structural EM,
none was less accurate in any setting of the
[0.16.0 benchmark](../docs/benchmarks/0.16.0.md#what-we-measured-on-missing-data). MVPC does not
help when each column's missingness depends weakly on many variables. With 50 or 100 variables
and 20 or 30% of the values missing, it made 0.7 to 3.6 more errors than test-wise deletion in 12
settings. With about 100 rows, hill climbing and Tabu search are often no better than the empty
graph, both with EM and on complete data. EM in the other searches is experimental, as no reference
implementation exists to compare them with.

**A path claim is read on the graph the search found.** In the methods that return a CPDAG, a
claim directs edges of that graph. Where the graph is wrong, a true claim can direct an edge the
wrong way. This happened to 1 to 11% of the edges true claims directed in our measurements.
A path claim also says less than an edge: it cannot restore an edge the search left out. When you
know the edge itself, state it as `required`. The path and confounder claims of the PAG methods
assume no selection bias: the samples were not kept or dropped by the value of a variable the
measured ones cause.

**The mixed-data tests are experimental.** When a column is a category, Andrey picks the conditional
Gaussian test. It runs on the survey, but we have not yet benchmarked it the way we have Fisher's z.
The 0.11.0 release card's results for missing values are on continuous data.

**A frequency is not a probability.** The bootstrap refits the method on resamples of the same
5,569 adults, so a frequency tells you how stable an edge is, not whether it is true. If the method
makes the same mistake every time, the wrong edge appears in every fit and gets a frequency of 1.
In the 0.11.0 simulations, the most frequent edges were almost always true, so the frequencies are
good for ranking edges. The averaged graph was not consistently more accurate than a single fit, so
we do not yet recommend it over one.

**The graph describes this sample and these columns.** NHANES samples some groups more than others
on purpose and gives each participant a weight to correct for it. We fit without the weights, so
the graph describes these 5,569 adults, not the US population. PC also assumes that no variable
left out of the table causes two that are in it. Diet and exercise are not in the table, yet they
can affect both BMI and blood pressure; PC could then draw an edge between those two that is really
the work of the missing variables. FCI allows for such hidden causes, and `confounded` tells it
which pairs share one.

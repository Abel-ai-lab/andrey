# Interventional data

## TL;DR

- **What it is:** data from experiments. Some rows are observed as they come; in others, a
  variable was changed from outside by an intervention. Where observation leaves the direction of
  an edge open, an experiment on either end can settle it.
- **What Andrey has:** GIES and IGSP when you know what each experiment changed, GnIES and UT-IGSP
  when you do not, CD-NOD for data from several domains, and ICP for the causes of one variable,
  all taking one `env` and `targets`, and a simulator for interventions.
- **As accurate as the authors' code:** GnIES is worse than the authors' package in none of 21
  settings and about 34 times faster; UT-IGSP is worse than the authors' library in none of 112
  comparisons; ICP accepts the same sets as the authors' R package on every paired fit with two
  environments, 41 to 1,554 times faster.

---

An experiment answers a question that observation cannot: what happens to the rest of a system
when one part of it is changed. Interventional data mix observed rows with rows from such
experiments, and they show which way an effect runs.

## Observational data

A [structural causal model](20261103-andrey-0-13-0.md#structural-causal-models) writes each
variable as a function of its parents and a noise term of its own. From observational data alone,
graphs that differ in the direction of some edges imply the same independences, so PC and GES
return those edges undirected. A functional causal model breaks the tie by assuming the shape of
each effect. Interventional data break it with an experiment.

## Interventions and the do operator

An intervention changes the mechanism of one variable, its target, from outside, and leaves every
other variable's mechanism as it was. Pearl writes a hard intervention with the do operator:
$\mathrm{do}(x_j = a)$ replaces the equation of $x_j$ with $x_j = a$, so $x_j$ no longer listens to
its parents, and the graph loses every arrow into $x_j$ (Pearl, 2009). A soft intervention changes
the mechanism and keeps the parents: it shifts the mean of the noise, changes its spread, or
changes the weights of the parents.

Intervening is not conditioning. $P(y \mid x = a)$ is the distribution of $y$ in the rows where $x$
happens to equal $a$, and it carries every path between $x$ and $y$, including a common cause.
$P(y \mid \mathrm{do}(x = a))$ is the distribution of $y$ under the intervention, and it changes
only through what $x$ causes. Observational data give the first; an experiment gives the second.

Data from several experiments come in environments: the rows of one environment share one set of
interventions, and its targets are the variables they change. An observational environment has no
targets.

Take the smallest graph, $x \to y$, with $y = 0.8\,x + e$ and $x$ and $e$ both Gaussian. The figure
draws the observed rows (a), and the rows of two experiments: $\mathrm{do}(x)$ (b) and
$\mathrm{do}(y)$ (c), each drawing its target from outside, centered at 2.5. Under (b) and (c), a
two-sample test asks whether the other variable changed.

```{raw} html
:file: 0.14.0/intervention.html
```

In (a), the data are a symmetric Gaussian cloud, and the models $x \to y$ and $y \to x$ fit them
alike. No method can tell the direction from these rows.

In (b), $\mathrm{do}(x)$ moves the cloud along the line: $y$ follows $x$, and the test finds that
$y$ changed. In (c), $\mathrm{do}(y)$ moves the cloud up, but $x$ keeps its place and spread, and
the test finds that $x$ did not change. An intervention changes what lies downstream of its target
and leaves the rest alone, so an experiment on either end of the edge orients it.

With experiments on several variables, the data identify the graph up to a smaller class, its
interventional equivalence class
(Hauser and Bühlmann, 2012): every edge at a target is directed, and so is every edge those
directions imply.

Under $\mathrm{do}(x_1 = 2)$, $x_1$ is 2.00 in every row, and GIES recovers the true graph.

`data` is a pandas DataFrame or a NumPy array, or the environments' tables apart. `env` and
`targets` take these forms:

| Argument | Form | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `env` | an array, one label per row | each row's environment; the labels are strings or numbers, not both |
| `env` | a column name, such as `"env"` | the DataFrame column that holds the labels; it is not a variable |
| `targets` | a mapping `{label: [variables]}` | each environment's targets: `[]` for an observational environment, a list for known targets, `None` for unknown ones |
| `targets` | a column name | a DataFrame column whose cells give each row's targets, empty for an observational row |
| `targets` | a 0/1 array of shape (rows, variables) | 1 where the row's environment intervenes on the variable |
| `data` | a list or dict of tables, one per environment | each environment's rows apart, labeled by position in a list and by key in a dict; `env` is left out, and `targets` maps those labels |

Variables are named by column label or 0-based position. With `targets` as a column or an array,
`env` can be left out: the rows with the same targets form one environment. GIES and IGSP need
every environment's targets; GnIES and UT-IGSP take `None` for the ones you do not know and learn
them; ICP needs no targets but uses them, leaving out the environments that intervened on its
response; CD-NOD takes `env` alone. GIES, GnIES, UT-IGSP, and CD-NOD also take the tables apart:

```python
andrey.gies([observed, knockout], targets={0: [], 1: ["x"]})
```

## Targets known and unknown

An experiment does not always say what it changed. A drug can act on more than the protein it was
made for, and a gene knockout can have side effects. Each method in Andrey assumes its own kind of
experiment and learns what it is not told:

| Method | Data | Targets | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| GIES | an observational environment and experiments | known | interventional essential graph |
| IGSP | an observational environment and experiments | known | interventional essential graph |
| GnIES | environments that differ in the spread of some variables' noise | learned | interventional essential graph and the targets |
| UT-IGSP | an observational environment and experiments | known in part, the rest learned | interventional essential graph and each experiment's targets |
| CD-NOD | domains whose mechanisms differ | not needed | CPDAG |
| ICP | environments that leave one variable's mechanism alone | not needed | that variable's causal parents |

**GIES** (Hauser and Bühlmann, 2012) runs GES's search over interventional equivalence classes,
scoring the rows of each environment with the targets left out of their own fit. **IGSP** (Wang,
Solus, Yang, and Uhler, 2017) searches variable orders for the sparsest graph the tests allow, and
uses each experiment to decide which directions it supports.

**GnIES** (Gamella, Taeb, Heinze-Deml, and Bühlmann, 2022) learns the targets with the graph, when
every experiment changes the spread of its targets' noise. It fits one set of targets for all
environments: the variables whose noise differs in some environment. **UT-IGSP** (Squires, Wang, and
Uhler, 2020) takes the targets you know and learns the rest, for each experiment, from tests of
which variables' mechanisms changed. It accepts any change of mechanism: a shift, a new spread, new
weights, or a hard intervention.

**CD-NOD** (Huang et al., 2020) reads the environment as one more variable that can cause the
others, and needs no targets. **ICP** (Peters, Bühlmann, and Meinshausen, 2016) asks a narrower
question: which variables are the causes of one response. It accepts the sets of variables whose
regression of the response stays the same in every environment, and returns the variables in all of
them.

## Simulating interventions

`andrey.data` draws interventional data. `hard(value)` is $\mathrm{do}(x_j = \text{value})$: it
fixes the variable and cuts it from its parents; `soft(shift=..., weight_scale=...,
noise_scale=...)` changes its mechanism and keeps its parents. `SCM.do` is the do operator: it
applies them to a model and returns the intervened model.
`envs=` on `SCM.sample` draws several environments, each with its own interventions, into one
dataset whose `env` and `targets` the methods take directly:

```python
import andrey.data as data
from andrey.data.interventions import hard, soft

scm = data.SCM(graph=data.graphs.erdos_renyi(d=8, avg_degree=2),
               functional=data.functional.linear(),
               noise=data.noise.gaussian())
ds = scm.sample(n=[500, 200, 200], seed=0, envs=[{}, {3: hard(2.0)}, {5: soft(shift=1.0)}])
ds.targets  # {0: [], 1: [3], 2: [5]}
```

## Checked against the authors' code

We compared each method with its authors' code on simulated data from the experiments it assumes.

- GnIES: the authors' gnies package. Worse in none of 21 settings, better in 2 on fresh data, and
  about 34 times faster.
- UT-IGSP: the authors' library. Worse in none of 112 comparisons. It is 1.7 times slower per fit
  at 20 variables; at 100 variables it takes 15 s against the library's 17 s.
- ICP: the authors' R package. The same accepted sets on all 5,220 paired fits with two
  environments, and every other difference traced to a documented choice. 41 to 1,554 times
  faster.

## Limits

**The experiments must fit the method.** GnIES assumes linear effects, Gaussian noise, and
experiments that change only the spread of their targets' noise; UT-IGSP and IGSP assume that the
observational environment is among the data.

**Few rows per experiment.** With 10 rows per environment, GnIES beats the empty graph in some
settings and not in others, and so does the authors' package.

**Dense graphs.** At 50 variables with mean degree 5, neither UT-IGSP nor the authors' library
makes fewer errors than the empty graph.

**Interventions on ICP's response.** ICP's guarantee needs environments that leave its response's
mechanism alone. Where an experiment hits the response, a set holding one of its children can look
invariant, and the share of fits that name a wrong parent rises to 0.18, in the authors' package
too. Pass the targets you know, and ICP leaves those environments out.

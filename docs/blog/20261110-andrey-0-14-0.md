# Interventional data

## TL;DR

- **What it is:** data from experiments. Some rows are observed as they come; in others, a
  variable was set or changed from outside. Where observation leaves the direction of an edge
  open, an experiment on either end can settle it.
- **What Andrey has:** GIES when you know what each experiment changed, GnIES and UT-IGSP when you
  do not, CD-NOD for data from several domains, and ICP for the causes of one variable, all taking
  one `env` and `targets`, and a simulator for interventions.
- **As accurate as the authors' code:** GnIES is worse than the authors' package in none of 21
  settings and about 34 times faster; UT-IGSP is worse than the authors' library in none of 112
  comparisons.

---

An experiment answers a question that observation cannot: what happens to the rest of a system
when one part of it is changed. Interventional data mix observed rows with rows from such
experiments, and they show which way an effect runs.

## Observational data

A [structural causal model](20261103-andrey-0-13-0.md#structural-causal-models) writes each
variable as a function of its parents and a noise term of its own. From observational data alone,
graphs that differ in the direction of some edges imply the same independences, so PC and GES
return those edges undirected. A functional causal model breaks the tie by assuming the shape of
each effect. Interventional data break it with an experiment.

## Interventions

An intervention changes the mechanism of one variable, its target, from outside. A hard
intervention sets the target and cuts it from its parents; a soft one changes its mechanism, such
as the mean or the spread of its noise, and keeps its parents. Every other variable keeps its
mechanism.

Take the smallest graph, $x \to y$, with $y = 0.8\,x + e$ and $x$ and $e$ both Gaussian. The figure
draws the observed rows (a), and the rows of two experiments: one that sets $x$ (b), and one that
sets $y$ (c). Under (b) and (c), a two-sample test asks whether the variable that was not set
changed.


In (a), the data are a symmetric Gaussian cloud, and the models $x \to y$ and $y \to x$ fit them
alike. No method can tell the direction from these rows.

In (b), setting $x$ moves the cloud along the line: $y$ follows $x$, and the test finds that $y$
changed. In (c), setting $y$ moves the cloud up, but $x$ keeps its place and spread, and the test
finds that $x$ did not change. An intervention changes what lies downstream of its target and
leaves the rest alone, so an experiment on either end of the edge orients it.

With experiments on several variables, the data identify the graph up to a smaller class, its
interventional equivalence class
([Hauser and Bühlmann, 2012](https://jmlr.org/papers/v13/hauser12a.html)): every edge at a target
is directed, and so is every edge those directions imply.

## Targets known and unknown

An experiment does not always say what it changed. A drug can act on more than the protein it was
made for, and a gene knockout can have side effects. Each method in Andrey assumes its own kind of
experiment and learns what it is not told:

| Method | Data | Targets | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| GIES | an observational environment and experiments | known | interventional essential graph |
| IGSP | an observational environment and experiments | known | interventional essential graph |
| GnIES | environments that differ in the spread of some variables' noise | learned | interventional essential graph and the targets |
| UT-IGSP | an observational environment and experiments | known in part, the rest learned | interventional essential graph and each experiment's targets |
| CD-NOD | domains whose mechanisms differ | not needed | CPDAG |
| ICP | environments that leave one variable's mechanism alone | not needed | that variable's causal parents |

**GIES** ([Hauser and Bühlmann, 2012](https://jmlr.org/papers/v13/hauser12a.html)) runs GES's
search over interventional equivalence classes, scoring the rows of each environment with the
targets left out of their own fit. **IGSP**
([Wang, Solus, Yang, and Uhler, 2017](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2017/hash/275d7fb2fd45098ad5c3ece2ed4a2824-Abstract.html))
searches variable orders for the sparsest graph the tests allow, and uses each experiment to decide
which directions it supports.

**GnIES**
([Gamella, Taeb, Heinze-Deml, and Bühlmann, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14897)) learns the
targets with the graph, when every experiment changes the spread of its targets' noise. It fits
one set of targets for all environments: the variables whose noise differs in some environment.
**UT-IGSP**
([Squires, Wang, and Uhler, 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v124/squires20a.html)) takes the
targets you know and learns the rest, for each experiment, from tests of which variables'
mechanisms changed. It accepts any change of mechanism: a shift, a new spread, new weights, or a
hard intervention.

**CD-NOD** ([Huang et al., 2020](https://jmlr.org/papers/v21/19-232.html)) reads the environment as
one more variable that can cause the others, and needs no targets. **ICP**
([Peters, Bühlmann, and Meinshausen, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/rssb.12167)) asks a narrower
question: which variables are the causes of one response. It accepts the sets of variables whose
regression of the response stays the same in every environment, and returns the variables in all
of them.

## One argument for environments

Every method that learns from environments takes `env`, the environment of each row, as an array or
the name of a column, and `targets`, the variables each environment intervened on. Map an
observational environment to `[]`, an experiment to the targets you know, and an experiment whose
targets you do not know to `None`:

```python
from andrey import ut_igsp

result = ut_igsp(data, "condition", targets={"control": [], "drug": ["dose"], "knockout": None})
result.metadata["targets"]  # each environment's targets, known and learned
```

## Simulating interventions

`andrey.data` draws interventional data. `hard(value)` sets a variable and cuts it from its
parents; `soft(shift=..., weight_scale=..., noise_scale=...)` changes its mechanism and keeps its
parents. `SCM.do` applies them to a model, and `envs=` on `SCM.sample` draws several environments,
each with its own interventions, into one dataset whose `env` and `targets` the methods take
directly:

```python
import andrey.data as data
from andrey.data.interventions import hard, soft

scm = data.SCM(graph=data.graphs.erdos_renyi(d=8, avg_degree=2),
               functional=data.functional.linear(),
               noise=data.noise.gaussian())
ds = scm.sample(n=[500, 200, 200], seed=0, envs=[{}, {3: hard(2.0)}, {5: soft(shift=1.0)}])
ds.targets  # {0: [], 1: [3], 2: [5]}
```

## Checked against the authors' code

We compared each method with its authors' code on simulated data from the experiments it assumes.

- GnIES: the authors' gnies package. Worse in none of 21 settings, better in 2 on fresh data, and
  about 34 times faster.
- UT-IGSP: the authors' library. Worse in none of 112 comparisons. It is 1.7 times slower per fit
  at 20 variables; at 100 variables it takes 15 s against the library's 17 s.

## Limits

**The experiments must fit the method.** GnIES assumes linear effects, Gaussian noise, and
experiments that change only the spread of their targets' noise; UT-IGSP and IGSP assume that the
observational environment is among the data.

**Few rows per experiment.** With 10 rows per environment, GnIES beats the empty graph in some
settings and not in others, and so does the authors' package.

**Dense graphs.** At 50 variables with mean degree 5, neither UT-IGSP nor the authors' library
makes fewer errors than the empty graph.

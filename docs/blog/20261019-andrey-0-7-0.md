# Permutation invariance

Give two people the same table with its columns in different orders. Ask each to learn a causal
graph. They should get the same graph. Column order carries no information: it comes from a database
export, a DataFrame, or whoever wrote the CSV. A method whose graph does not depend on that order is
permutation invariant.

Strictly, such a method is permutation equivariant: reorder the columns, and the graph's nodes
reorder the same way. Here, "the same graph" allows for that relabeling.

Many causal-discovery methods are not invariant. When two steps look equally good, they take the
first one they find, and "first" means the leftmost column.
[Colombo and Maathuis (2014)](https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/colombo14a.html) showed this for PC. It
matters for three reasons:

- **Reproducibility.** Two analysts with the same data get different graphs.
- **Trust.** An edge that flips when you reorder the columns was never supported by the data.
- **Benchmarks.** Data generators often write columns in an order tied to the graph, so a method
  that favors the first column gains or loses accuracy it did not earn.

In six of our methods, we replace choices tied to column order with choices tied to the data. Our
benchmark also shuffles columns to keep their order from skewing the results.

## TL;DR

- Andrey 0.7.0 removes permutation variance: reorder your columns, and PC, hill climbing, tabu
  search, BOSS, GRaSP, and GIN return the same graph.
- The same methods in other packages change their graphs when you reorder the columns.
- Our benchmark data now come with shuffled columns, so column order cannot skew our measurements.

## How we found it

It started with hill climbing. On the 0.6.0 categorical benchmark, pgmpy's hill climbing had fewer
errors than Andrey's on 17 datasets. We went looking for a score bug. We found a tie. Under BDeu and
BIC, adding `x -> y` and adding `y -> x` can score exactly the same. Andrey gave the tie to
whichever column came first. That decided every one of the 17. Reverse the columns, and Andrey had
no more errors than pgmpy on 14 of them.
[Kitson and Constantinou (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08952) found that variable order moves
hill climbing's graphs more than many other choices do.

Then we checked our data generators. They wrote columns in an order tied to the graph: on a
scale-free graph, the biggest hubs came first. A method that breaks ties by that order gains or
loses accuracy just from the column layout.
[Reisach, Seiler, and Weichwald (2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.13647) found a related artifact
in simulated data: variance that grows along the causal order. Ours was simpler: the column order
itself followed the graph.

That order misled us. On scale-free graphs, the new PC rule looked worse than the old one by 1.0 and
4.8 endpoint errors on average at the two significance levels. Shuffle the same columns, and it was
better by 1.0 and 3.1. Below, each node carries its column number, before and after our generators
shuffle the columns. Beside the graphs, you can see the result reverse.

## Why it happens

Column order enters when a method takes the first of two equally good steps, or starts from the
columns' positions. For PC, five variables are enough to show why.

We generated 300 rows in which A causes B, C, and D, and each of them causes E. PC orients a
collider `x -> y <- z` when `x` and `z` look independent and `y` did not separate them. Here, B, C,
and D look independent given A, so PC proposes three true colliders at E. But A and E also look
independent given B, with a p-value of 0.052, just above the significance level of 0.05. PC then
proposes `A -> C <- E` and `A -> D <- E`, pointing the edges between E and C and between E and D the
wrong way.

When colliders disagree about an edge, the first one PC orients claims it. PC skips the later one.
The column-order rule visits colliders by the column they point into, left to right. Put C or D
before E, and a false collider claims its edge first. Across the 120 column orders, the rule returns
4 different graphs, finding the true one on only 40. The p-value rule starts with the colliders
whose pair was most clearly separated, so E's colliders always win.

Click a column to move it first, or shuffle them all. Put E first, and the true graph appears; put C
or D before it, and an edge flips.


We found the same pattern in five places:

- **PC.** When two colliders disagree about one edge, PC kept whichever came first in column order,
  and Meek's rules, which orient the remaining edges, fired in column order too. On a seeded grid,
  the output changed on 116 of 150 reorderings.
- **Hill climbing and tabu search.** An exact score tie went to the column that came first.
- **BOSS and GRaSP.** Both search over orders of the variables. BOSS started from the column order
  and visited variables in a seeded shuffle of their positions; GRaSP shuffled the positions for its
  starting order and its lists of parents. With the same seed, a reordering changed BOSS's graph on
  146 of 150 linear-Gaussian datasets and GRaSP's on 149.
- **GIN.** The root test, which decides which hidden cause comes first, built each cluster's
  stand-in from the cluster's first column.
- **FCI.** The possible-d-separation step visits nodes in column order. On graphs with 50 to 200
  variables, reordering the columns changed FCI's result on 51% of the reorderings we tried.

## How we fixed it

We replace a column's position with a value computed from its data. That value follows the column
when you move it.

### PC

PC orients conflicting colliders by the p-value that separated each collider's pair, highest first.
We adapted this rule from [Ramsey (2016)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.00378);
[Colombo and Maathuis (2014)](https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/colombo14a.html) give the background. The
collider step no longer depends on column order. `collider_order="column"` keeps the old rule.

Meek's rules then run in rounds. Each round applies every orientation they imply at once; an edge
they would direct both ways stays undirected, and so does one that would close a directed cycle.
When the colliders fit a DAG, this gives the same graph as firing the rules one at a time, and in
every case it does not depend on the order.

We compared the two rules on shuffled data across 16 settings and 1,040 datasets. These cover
linear-Gaussian data on Erdos-Renyi and scale-free graphs with 10 to 200 variables, Student-t noise,
and the categorical and mixed data settings of the 0.6.0 release card.

- **Order.** With the p-value rule, no reordering changed PC's graph in any of the 16 settings.
  With the column-order rule, 40% to 95% did.
- **Errors.** The p-value rule has fewer endpoint errors on average in all 16 settings. The
  difference is clear in 11; in the four categorical settings and one mixed setting, its 95%
  interval includes zero.
- **Time.** The p-value rule takes at most about 4% longer.

### Hill climbing and tabu search

Hill climbing and tabu search break exact score ties with a rank computed from each column's data.
For numeric columns, we use the signs of changes from row to row; for categorical columns, which
rows share a category. The rank follows the column, and small rounding differences between machines
do not change it. We fit both methods on 150 categorical datasets with 10, 20, and 40 variables,
each in its own column order and five reorderings, and counted the datasets where a reordering
changed the graph.

| Method | Score | 0.6.0 | 0.7.0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hill climbing | BDeu | 145 of 150 | 0 of 150 |
| Hill climbing | BIC | 147 of 150 | 0 of 150 |
| Tabu search | BDeu | 112 of 150 | 0 of 150 |
| Tabu search | BIC | 118 of 150 | 0 of 150 |

Accuracy stays the same. For each method and score, the change in mean errors from 0.6.0 is between
-0.06 and -0.26, and every 95% interval includes zero. Hill climbing's median is 9.0 errors in both
releases.

### BOSS and GRaSP

BOSS and GRaSP sort the columns by the same rank before searching. Their starting order, seeded
shuffles, and score ties then follow each column's data. Different seeds still give different
graphs, and a seed's graph can differ from 0.6.0's. We fit both methods in Andrey and in
causal-learn on 150 linear-Gaussian datasets on Erdos-Renyi graphs with 10, 20, and 50 variables,
each in its own column order and five reorderings, and counted the datasets where a reordering
changed the graph.

| Method | causal-learn | Andrey 0.6.0 | Andrey 0.7.0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| BOSS | 137 of 150 | 146 of 150 | 0 of 150 |
| GRaSP | 150 of 150 | 149 of 150 | 0 of 150 |

We compare accuracy with causal-learn's BOSS and GRaSP on the same datasets and column orders, at
the same penalty, rather than with 0.6.0, whose errors partly came from the column order. Andrey's
errors are indistinguishable from causal-learn's: the paired difference from causal-learn's mean
over the six orders is +0.01 errors for BOSS (95% interval -0.99 to +1.01) and +0.84 for GRaSP
(-0.78 to +2.46).

### GIN

GIN's root test runs once per member of the clusters it compares, then pools the p-values. The
search visits columns sorted by the same rank. On the 15 benchmark datasets with 2 to 4 hidden
causes, ten reorderings of the columns change the latent order on none, against 11 of 15 with the
first-column test. The clusters never changed.

### FCI

`fci` and `gfci` accept `possible_dsep="order_free"`. This option builds each possible-d-separation
set after the search instead of while it runs. On graphs with 50 to 200 variables, it lowers the
share of reorderings that change FCI's result from 34% to 16%. Endpoint errors change by less than
half a percent. That step can run up to 4.61 times as many tests. The option is off by default.

Simulated data can understate the problem. We also tried both versions on six real tables, each
under 200 random column orders (20 for the full superconductivity table). We counted how many orders
changed the result and how many different graphs appeared, including the original order's graph.

| Dataset | Variables | Rows | Changed orders | Graphs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sachs protein signaling | 11 | 853 | 0 / 0 of 200 | 1 / 1 |
| Wine | 13 | 178 | 0 / 0 of 200 | 1 / 1 |
| Diabetes | 10 | 442 | 150 / 101 of 200 | 4 / 2 |
| Breast cancer | 30 | 569 | 156 / 156 of 200 | 3 / 3 |
| Superconductivity, 1,000-row sample | 81 | 1,000 | 129 / 134 of 200 | 3 / 3 |
| Superconductivity | 81 | 21,263 | 20 / 20 of 20 | 19 / 21 |

Each cell gives the default first and `possible_dsep="order_free"` second. We used the observational
measurements of Sachs et al. (2005); the wine, diabetes, and breast cancer tables bundled with
scikit-learn; and the UCI superconductivity table (Hamidieh, 2018).

Column order never mattered on Sachs and wine. On the other four tables it still changed the result,
through the possible-d-separation step, though on most of them a reordering led to only a few
different graphs. The order-free sets helped on diabetes, did slightly worse on the
superconductivity sample, and made little difference on the rest.

### The benchmark data

Our data generators shuffle the columns along with the true graph (`andrey.data.sample_scm`,
`sample_discrete`, and `sample_mixed`; `shuffle=False` keeps the drawn order).
`andrey.data.load_dataset` does the same for bnlearn networks, whose files list variables close to
causal order. We also shuffle the inputs for our recorded test outputs and example notebooks. Every
benchmark from 0.7.0 uses these generators. Earlier published summaries keep their numbers.

## How Andrey compares

Permutation invariance is a yes-or-no property, so a few small examples answer it. We fit each
package's implementation at its defaults on three small datasets per method, each in nine column
orders, and checked whether any reordering changed the graph. The datasets had 8 linear-Gaussian
variables and 500 rows, 7 categorical variables and 1,000 rows for hill climbing and tabu search,
or two or three hidden causes with three indicators each for GIN.

```{raw} html
:file: 0.7.0/compare.html
```

For BOSS, GRaSP, GIN, and tabu search, every other implementation we checked changed its graph under
some reordering, and Andrey's did not. bnlearn's hill climbing changed too, and pgmpy's changed
between runs. Andrey's PC returned the same graph on every example; causal-learn's, pcalg's, and
bnlearn's changed, and pgmpy's and Tetrad's follow the variables' names.

Two packages can fix PC's collider step with an option that is off by default: causal-learn's
`uc_priority=1`, and pcalg's `maj.rule=TRUE` with `solve.confl=TRUE`. Ranking colliders by p-value
needs care with exact ties. causal-learn's `uc_priority=3` also ranks by p-value, but on an example
where two p-values tie at 0, it falls back to column order and returned 2 graphs from 24 orders.
Andrey's PC returned 1. GES returned the same graph under every reordering in every package we
checked. At their defaults, FCI changed in causal-learn, pcalg, and Andrey, and Tetrad's follows the
names.

Agreement with another package is a measurement, not the goal. On the seven settings where we also
ran causal-learn, which keeps the column-order rule, the new PC rule matches its graph on 6% to 60%
of datasets, against 75% to 100% with the old rule. Yet it makes fewer errors.

## Where order still enters

FCI's and GFCI's orientation rules run in rounds like Meek's, so they do not depend on the order.
Their possible-d-separation step still can: `possible_dsep="order_free"` removes the order from it
only when the tests are exact, and on sampled data their result can still depend on column order.

For the same graph on every machine and every run, sort your columns by name before fitting.

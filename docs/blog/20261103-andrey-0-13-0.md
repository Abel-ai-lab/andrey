# Functional causal models

## TL;DR

- **What it is:** a functional causal model writes each variable as a function of its causes plus
  independent noise. When the function or the noise has the right shape, only the true direction
  fits the data.
- **What Andrey has:** DirectLiNGAM and ICA-LiNGAM for linear effects, ANM and PNL for a pairwise
  relationship, and CAM and RESIT for nonlinear effects across a whole graph, with experimental
  CAM-UV for hidden variables. New in 0.17.0, SCORE and NoGAM read the causal order of nonlinear
  data from the score, the gradient of its log density.
- **As accurate as the authors' code, and faster:** in the 0.13.0 benchmarks, CAM makes no more
  errors than CAM 1.0 in any of 16 settings and is 6.5 to 14.7 times faster; RESIT makes no more
  errors than its authors' code in any of 12 and is 3.2 to 4.6 times faster. In the 0.17.0
  benchmarks, SCORE and NoGAM make no more errors than their reference implementations in any
  setting.

---

Most causal discovery methods can tell which variables are connected, but not always which way.
Functional causal models read the direction from the shape of an effect, even when the effect is a
curve.

## Structural causal models

A structural causal model writes each variable as a function of its parents (its direct causes)
and a noise term of its own:

$$x_j = f_j(\mathrm{pa}_j, e_j), \qquad j = 1, \dots, d,$$

with the noise terms independent of each other. The graph draws an arrow from each parent to its
child, and the data are draws of all the $x_j$.

When nothing restricts the functions $f_j$ or the noise, the data reveal only which variables are
independent given which others. Graphs that differ in the direction of some edges imply the same
independences, so PC and GES, which read independences, return those edges undirected.

## Functional causal models

Take the smallest graph, $x \to y$, with $y = f(x) + e$. One way to find its direction is to fit
each variable from the other and test whether what is left, the residual, is independent of the
variable it was fit on. In the true direction, the residual is the noise $e$, which is independent
of $x$. The figure runs this test on three pairs, (a) to (c), one per column. The top row shows the
data, the middle row the residual of a fit of $y$ from $x$, and the bottom row the residual of a fit
of $x$ from $y$. Under each residual is the verdict of a kernel independence test (HSIC).

```{raw} html
:file: 0.17.0/direction.html
```

In (a), $y = 0.8\,x + e$ with $x$ and $e$ both Gaussian, and the data form a symmetric Gaussian
cloud. Both residuals are shapeless clouds, and the test finds both independent. The reverse model
$x = b\,y + e'$ fits the data as well as the true one, so no method can tell the direction from
these data.

In (b), the line is the same, but $x$ and $e$ are uniform. The residual of $y$ from $x$ is still
shapeless, but the residual of $x$ from $y$ forms a diamond: it spreads widest at middle values of
$y$ and narrows at the ends, so it depends on $y$. Only the true direction fits. This is what LiNGAM
methods use: linear effects with non-Gaussian noise.

In (c), $y = 0.5\,x^3 - x + e$, with Gaussian noise again. The residual of $y$ from $x$ is
shapeless, but the residual of $x$ from $y$ follows the bends of the curve, and the test finds it
dependent. This is what CAM and RESIT use: nonlinear effects.

A functional causal model restricts the form of each $f_j$ and its noise so that, as in (b) and
(c), only the true direction leaves a residual independent of the cause
([Hoyer et al., 2008](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/3548-nonlinear-causal-discovery-with-additive-noise-models)).
Each functional causal model in Andrey assumes its own form of $f_j$:

| Method | Model of each variable | The direction comes from | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| DirectLiNGAM, ICA-LiNGAM | $x_j = \sum_{k \in \mathrm{pa}_j} b_{jk}\, x_k + e_j$, with $e_j$ non-Gaussian | the shape of the noise | DAG |
| ANM | $y = f(x) + e$ | a nonlinear $f$ | pairwise relationship |
| PNL | $y = g(f(x) + e)$, with $g$ invertible | a nonlinear $f$ and $g$ | pairwise relationship |
| CAM | $x_j = \sum_{k \in \mathrm{pa}_j} f_{jk}(x_k) + e_j$ | a nonlinear $f_{jk}$ | DAG and causal order |
| RESIT | $x_j = f_j(\mathrm{pa}_j) + e_j$, with $e_j$ of any distribution | a nonlinear $f_j$ | DAG and causal order |
| SCORE | $x_j = f_j(\mathrm{pa}_j) + e_j$, with $e_j$ Gaussian | a nonlinear $f_j$ | DAG and causal order |
| NoGAM | $x_j = f_j(\mathrm{pa}_j) + e_j$, with $e_j$ of any distribution | a nonlinear $f_j$ | DAG and causal order |
| CAM-UV, experimental | CAM's model, with some variables hidden | a nonlinear $f_{jk}$ | PAG, `o-o` where a hidden variable joins a pair |

The LiNGAM methods, CAM, RESIT, SCORE, and NoGAM form one family in Andrey, "Functional causal
models"; ANM and PNL, which take one pair at a time, are under "Pairwise". Since 0.16.0, LOCI joins
them for a pair whose noise changes with the cause; the
[pairwise direction post](20261015-andrey-0-4-0.md) describes it.

**CAM** ([Bühlmann, Peters, and Ernest, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1214/14-AOS1260)) makes each
effect a sum of one smooth function per parent and scores a graph by the Gaussian likelihood of
its residuals. It picks each variable's possible parents, adds the edges that most reduce the
residual variance, and prunes the smooth terms that are not significant. A score is cheap to
compute, so CAM reaches 100 variables.

**RESIT**
([Peters, Mooij, Janzing, and Schölkopf, 2014](https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/peters14a.html))
assumes nothing about the noise distribution. It builds the causal order from the last variable
up, each time taking the variable whose residual a kernel test finds most independent of all the
remaining variables. It then drops each parent whose removal keeps the residual independent.
Andrey fits each $f_j$ as a sum of smooth terms, as CAM does. Each test reads all the remaining
variables at once, so RESIT suits a handful of them.

**CAM-UV** ([Maeda and Shimizu, 2021](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v161/maeda21a.html)) keeps
CAM's model and allows hidden variables. Where a hidden common cause or a hidden step on a causal
path joins two variables, no regression can tell their relation, and it marks the pair `o-o`
instead of guessing.

## SCORE and NoGAM

New in 0.17.0, SCORE and NoGAM fit the model of RESIT, $x_j = f_j(\mathrm{pa}_j) + e_j$, and read
the causal order from the score of the data, the gradient of its log density,
$\nabla \log p(x)$.

**SCORE** ([Rolland et al., 2022](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/rolland22a.html)) assumes
Gaussian noise. Under that model, a variable has no children exactly when the derivative of its
entry of the score, $\partial^2 \log p(x) / \partial x_j^2$, is the same at every point. SCORE
estimates these derivatives at the rows with a kernel estimator. It takes the variable whose
derivative varies least as the last in the order, removes it, and repeats on the others.

**NoGAM** ([Montagna et al., 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v213/montagna23a.html)) allows
noise of any distribution. A variable has no children exactly when its entry of the score is a
function of its noise alone, and that noise is what a regression of the variable on all the others
leaves. NoGAM predicts each entry of the score from that residual and takes the variable predicted
with the smallest error as the last in the order.

Both then prune the complete DAG of the order with CAM's tests of its smooth terms, and both return
a DAG and a causal order.

Use SCORE when the effects are nonlinear and the noise is Gaussian, and NoGAM when you cannot assume
a noise distribution. In the 0.17.0 benchmarks, on the simulation of their papers with four times as
many edges as variables, both ordered as many true edges as CAM or more. On sparse graphs, with as
many edges as variables, CAM is more accurate. Neither method suits every additive-noise model,
as the example shows.

## Checked against the authors' code

We compared each method with the reference code on simulated data from the model it assumes. In the
0.13.0 benchmarks, CAM, RESIT, and CAM-UV make no more errors than their reference code, and run
faster:

- CAM: CAM 1.0, 6.5 to 14.7 times faster.
- RESIT: the authors' R code, 3.2 to 4.6 times faster; lingam, 2.1 to 2.9 times faster, with fewer
  errors in 11 of 12 settings.
- CAM-UV: lingam, 2.7 to 3.7 times faster; causal-learn, 7.5 to 16 times faster.

In the 0.17.0 benchmarks, on the simulation of their papers, SCORE and NoGAM make no more errors
than theirs:

- SCORE: its authors' code, 4 to 9 times faster.
- NoGAM: dodiscover, 1.6 to 3 times faster, with fewer errors in 16 of 24 settings.

The [0.13.0 release card](../docs/benchmarks/0.13.0.md) and the
[0.17.0 release card](../docs/benchmarks/0.17.0.md#score-and-nogam) have every comparison.

## Simulating functional causal models

`andrey.data.sample_scm` draws data from these models, with `functional` naming the form of each
$f_j$: `"linear"`, `"additive_noise"` (the example above), `"post_nonlinear"`, and
`"location_scale"`, whose noise scale depends on the parents. The last breaks the model of CAM
and RESIT. We use it to see how they fail when their model does not hold.

## Limits

**The model must fit.** CAM and RESIT assume nonlinear effects with additive noise and no hidden
common causes. With linear effects and Gaussian noise, the data identify only the equivalence
class; noise whose scale depends on the parents can reverse edges.

**SCORE and NoGAM do not suit every additive-noise model.** Their accuracy holds on the simulation
of their papers. On Andrey's own additive-noise simulation, both order the variables close to at
random, as their reference implementations do, and CAM is the method to use. SCORE's rule is exact
under Gaussian noise only, and NoGAM was measured at 10 and 20 variables.

**RESIT is for small graphs.** Its accuracy claim holds up to 10 variables. At 20, on Gaussian
noise, no implementation we measured clearly beats the empty graph.

**Many hidden variables defeat CAM-UV.** Where hidden variables carry most of the edges, neither
CAM-UV nor the reference implementations make fewer errors than the empty graph, and all of them
mark few of the pairs a hidden variable joins.

::::{div} release-opening-text

0.17.0 adds SCORE and NoGAM, two methods for nonlinear data with additive noise. Each reads a causal
order from the score of the data, the gradient of its log density, and then prunes the order to a
DAG. SCORE assumes Gaussian noise, and NoGAM works under additive noise of any distribution. Both
are supported. NOTEARS, experimental until now, is supported too.

::::
:::::

## The benchmarks

The earlier release cards keep the numbers of the builds they measured. This release's summary adds
the comparison of SCORE with its authors' code and of NoGAM with dodiscover ([SCORE and
NoGAM](#score-and-nogam)), which the homepage shows beside them, and the comparison of NOTEARS with
its authors' code ([NOTEARS](#notears)).

## SCORE and NoGAM

`andrey.score_matching` is SCORE (Rolland et al., 2022), and `andrey.nogam` is NoGAM (Montagna et
al., 2023). Both find a causal order from the leaf up with a kernel estimate of the score and prune
the complete DAG of that order with the tests of `cam`. They return a DAG and a causal order, take
`knowledge`, and give the same result in any units and column order. The [API
reference](../api.md#supported-methods) describes each, and the [functional causal models
post](../../blog/20261103-andrey-0-13-0.md) explains the model they share with CAM and RESIT.

We compared SCORE with its authors' code and NoGAM with dodiscover on the simulation of their
papers, on 20 datasets per setting, with every column z-scored and with each variable rescaled: 12
settings for SCORE and 24 for NoGAM. Neither method made more errors (SHD) than its reference in any
setting, and neither ordered fewer true edges in a setting that repeated on fresh datasets. NoGAM
made fewer errors than dodiscover in 16 of its 24 settings, on the first datasets and again on 20
fresh ones, and ordered more true edges in 18. SCORE's median fit was 4 to 9 times faster than its
authors' code, and NoGAM's 1.6 to 3 times faster than dodiscover. The [comparison page][sn] has
every setting and number.

The table shows z-scored data with 1,000 rows. SHD is the mean number of edge errors, and the empty
graph's SHD is the number of edges. Order share is the share of true edges that the causal order
puts in the true direction.

| Method, noise | Variables | Edges | SHD: Andrey | Reference | Order share: Andrey | Reference | Fit, s: Andrey | Reference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| SCORE, Gaussian | 10 | 10 | 2.8 | 3.1 | 0.81 | 0.79 | 1.0 | 4.0 |
| SCORE, Gaussian | 10 | 40 | 22.1 | 22.4 | 0.97 | 0.96 | 0.9 | 4.0 |
| SCORE, Gaussian | 20 | 20 | 7.8 | 8.2 | 0.73 | 0.71 | 3.0 | 14.3 |
| SCORE, Gaussian | 20 | 80 | 51.9 | 51.5 | 0.90 | 0.91 | 2.7 | 14.1 |
| SCORE, Gaussian | 50 | 50 | 25.0 | 24.4 | 0.65 | 0.66 | 28.2 | 262.3 |
| SCORE, Gaussian | 50 | 200 | 142.7 | 141.2 | 0.85 | 0.87 | 32.4 | 251.8 |
| NoGAM, Laplace | 10 | 10 | 1.2 | 2.9 | 0.90 | 0.77 | 9.4 | 20.4 |
| NoGAM, Laplace | 10 | 40 | 19.9 | 20.4 | 0.98 | 0.98 | 9.7 | 23.7 |
| NoGAM, Laplace | 20 | 20 | 4.2 | 5.8 | 0.86 | 0.81 | 37.9 | 79.2 |
| NoGAM, Laplace | 20 | 80 | 48.8 | 51.1 | 0.94 | 0.93 | 37.8 | 108.3 |

- On sparse graphs CAM is more accurate. With as many edges as variables, SCORE's order put 65% to
  81% of the true edges in the true direction and NoGAM's 81% to 92%, where CAM's put 90% to 98%.
  With four times as many edges, both ordered as many true edges as CAM or more.
- Both papers report data in the simulation's own units, where a variable's variance grows along the
  causal order. Andrey standardizes the columns before the order search, so its results are below
  the papers' tables on sparse graphs.
- At 50 variables with four times as many edges, SCORE's order share was 0.007 to 0.010 below its
  authors' code over 40 datasets, 0.86 against 0.87. The setting was not worse again on the fresh
  datasets alone.
- SCORE's rule is exact under Gaussian noise only. NoGAM was measured at 10 and 20 variables, and a
  single fit at 50 variables took about three minutes.
- Neither method carries over to every additive-noise model. On Andrey's own additive-noise
  simulation, with links `z + sin z`, SCORE ordered 41% to 53% of the true edges correctly and NoGAM
  47% to 62%, close to a random order, and at 20 variables both returned a graph with more errors
  than the empty graph. CAM ordered 76% to 94% there, and the reference implementations do as SCORE
  and NoGAM do. The cause is the estimators' error on a variable, which grows with the variable's
  variance relative to its noise: links with a strong linear part build up variance along the causal
  order, so the sinks get the largest errors and both searches rank them last, and 16,000 rows did
  not change that. Use CAM on such data.

## NOTEARS

`andrey.notears` is NOTEARS (Zheng et al., 2018). It fits a weighted DAG to linear data by
minimizing a least-squares loss with an `l1` penalty under a smooth acyclicity constraint, and it
returns a DAG with the coefficient of each edge. The [API reference](../api.md#supported-methods)
describes it.

We compared it with its authors' code on the simulation of the paper: linear models on Erdos-Renyi
graphs with twice as many edges as variables and on scale-free graphs with four times as many, with
10 to 100 variables, Gaussian, exponential, or Gumbel noise, 1,000 rows, and every column z-scored,
on 20 datasets per setting. In those 24 settings NOTEARS made no more errors (SHD) than the authors'
code: the two tied in 23, and NOTEARS made fewer in 1, which we did not rerun. It made fewer errors
than the empty graph in 15 of the 24, and the authors' code in 16. The [comparison page][nd] has
every setting and number, and those of DAGMA, which stays experimental.

The table shows z-scored data with Gaussian noise. SHD is the mean number of edge errors, and the
empty graph's SHD is the number of edges. Same graph counts the datasets on which both return the
same DAG, and the fit time is the median.

| Graphs | Variables | Edges | SHD: Andrey | Reference | Same graph | Fit, s: Andrey | Reference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Erdos-Renyi | 10 | 20 | 18.4 | 18.3 | 18 of 20 | 2.4 | 1.5 |
| Erdos-Renyi | 20 | 40 | 37.4 | 37.5 | 15 of 20 | 7.6 | 8.7 |
| Erdos-Renyi | 50 | 100 | 88.0 | 87.9 | 10 of 20 | 40.3 | 63.4 |
| Erdos-Renyi | 100 | 200 | 157.2 | 156.7 | 7 of 20 | 239.9 | 392.4 |
| Scale-free | 10 | 24 | 23.1 | 23.3 | 15 of 20 | 2.0 | 1.5 |
| Scale-free | 20 | 64 | 64.2 | 64.0 | 10 of 20 | 10.8 | 13.7 |
| Scale-free | 50 | 184 | 180.8 | 181.0 | 8 of 20 | 38.3 | 63.3 |
| Scale-free | 100 | 384 | 379.4 | 379.9 | 2 of 20 | 327.8 | 467.9 |

- On the scale-free graphs NOTEARS was within 5% of the empty graph in all 12 settings and made
  fewer errors than it in 3; on the Erdos-Renyi graphs it made 6% to 22% fewer in all 12. In one
  scale-free setting, 50 variables with exponential noise, the authors' code made fewer errors than
  the empty graph and NOTEARS did not, at the same mean SHD (181.3 against 184). On 40 fresh
  datasets of that setting neither did: NOTEARS had 184.8 errors and the authors' code 184.3, a tie
  (the difference was 0.45, with a 95% interval from -0.1 to 1.0).
- On data in the simulation's own units, where a variable's variance grows along the causal order,
  NOTEARS made far fewer errors: 2.5 to 83.5 against the empty graph's 20 to 184, at 10 to 50
  variables. Sorting the columns by variance and regressing each on those before it made 4.3 to
  150 on the same data, so most of that accuracy comes from the data's units.
- On z-scored data the thresholded weights are often cyclic: those of the authors' code were in 79
  of 120 fits at 20 variables and 89 of 120 at 50. Both sides then drop the weakest edges until no
  cycle is left, a median of 12 of about 59 edges at 50 variables, so the comparison is of the two
  fits with that repair.
- The fit ends at a local solution that the last bits of the input can change. At 50 variables,
  changing every value by a relative 1e-12 gave another graph in 47 of 100 fits and moved the
  median SHD by at most 1, so a graph can differ between machines where the errors do not.
- On sparser graphs, with as many edges as variables, `ges` and `calm` are more accurate. On
  z-scored columns at 10 to 100 variables, the median SHD against the true CPDAG was 7 to 92 for
  NOTEARS, 2 to 79 for `ges`, and 3.5 to 36 for `calm`.
- At 10 variables NOTEARS's median fit was slower than the authors' code, at 0.67 to 0.79 times its
  speed. From 20 variables on it was 1.1 to 1.7 times faster, and at 100 variables a fit took a
  median of about four to six minutes.

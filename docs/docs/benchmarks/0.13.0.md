# 0.13.0 release card: functional causal models

:::::{div} release-opening

Functional causal models

::::{div} release-opening-text

This card measures the four methods 0.13.0 makes supported: CAM and RESIT against their authors'
code, and IAMB and MMPC against bnlearn, on linear-Gaussian data and on five discrete networks.
Each result holds for the measured conditions; it depends on the data, settings, and hardware.

::::
:::::

IAMB, MMPC, CAM, and RESIT are supported methods. We compare IAMB and MMPC with bnlearn 5.2.1 on the
graph each returns, scored against that graph's own truth: the CPDAG for `structure="cpdag"`, the
skeleton for `structure="skeleton"`, the default of MMPC, and the moral graph for
`structure="moral"`, which bnlearn's `learn.mb` blankets joined by the AND rule give. Three grids of
linear-Gaussian data measure them: standardized columns, columns left in their own units, and 200
variables. The same two methods, Inter-IAMB, MMHC, and RSMAX2 are then compared on five discrete
networks (asia, child, insurance, alarm, and hepar2). There each package runs its own tests:
Andrey's calibrated chi-square and G-square tests (`indep_test="chisq"` and `"gsq"`) against
bnlearn's `x2-adf` and `mi-adf`. The hybrids score with BDeu, equivalent sample size 1, in both
packages. CAM is compared with CAM 1.0, the R package of its authors, on nonlinear additive-noise
data, the model it assumes, and then on two kinds of data outside that model. RESIT is compared with
the authors' R code and with lingam's implementation on the same kinds of data, at 5 to 20
variables.

**IAMB on linear-Gaussian data.** The two unstandardized settings at 20 variables and mean degree 4
do not replicate: the rerun's D is -0.10 on both, and 7 and 9 of its 10 graphs are identical. The
standardized setting with 50 variables, 5,000 rows, and mean degree 2 is worse (16 of its 20 graphs
are identical). Most of its gap, +7 pairs of the +10 over its 20 datasets, sits in 2 datasets whose
skeletons differ, and the skeleton difference starts at one node's first grow step, where the
p-values of several candidates underflow to exactly 0 in bnlearn, which takes the first column among
them. Andrey takes the candidate with the strongest test statistic, the association measure of
Tsamardinos, Aliferis, and Statnikov (2003), so its result does not depend on the column order. The
choice bnlearn makes, applied in Andrey's search, reproduces bnlearn's skeleton in all 4 traced
datasets. The other +3 are orientation differences in 2 datasets with equal skeletons: Meek's rules
on a cyclic partially directed graph, and a collider that bnlearn skips for closing a directed
cycle. The skeleton of the same cell is a tie, and so is its CPDAG on unstandardized data (-0.20
[-0.60, +0.00]).

**MMPC, skeleton at 200 variables.** The skeleton at 200 variables, 5,000 rows, and mean degree 2 is
worse (D +1.60 [+0.10, +3.10] on seeds 0-9 and +2.70 [+1.20, +4.40] on seeds 20-29). At this size
MMPC's forward step often faces candidates whose dependence is too strong to rank by p-value, and
Andrey admits the one with the strongest test statistic, the association measure of Tsamardinos,
Brown, and Aliferis (2006), which is a true neighbor in 2,376 of 2,384 first picks (99.7%); the
first column, bnlearn's pick, is one in 49%. The extra errors are false edges between unrelated
pairs whose statistic is just past the cutoff (z 2.0 to 3.1, the cutoff is 1.96); an arbitrary pick
happens to admit a strongly correlated non-neighbor that removes some of them. Strongest-first keeps
44 such edges that the other order removes, about 2 per graph.

**MMPC, CPDAG at 100 variables.** The setting with 100 variables, 1,000 rows, and mean degree 2 is
worse on seeds 0-19 (D +0.70 [+0.10, +1.55]). Its rerun on seeds 20-29 has 9 paired datasets, since
bnlearn did not finish one in 600 s, so it is not judged: the mean D is +0.56 (an interval of -0.22
to +1.78), 6 of the 9 graphs are identical, and the whole difference sits in 3 datasets (-1, +1,
+5). The setting counts as worse.

**IAMB on hepar2.** The moral graph of hepar2 at 5,000 rows is worse on both runs (D +5.81 [+1.81,
+9.63] on seeds 0-19 and +10.30 [+5.20, +15.20] on seeds 20-29). The two packages run different
discrete tests: Andrey's chi-square test is calibrated by the exact permutation mean of the
statistic as its degrees of freedom (Tsamardinos and Borboudakis 2010), and bnlearn's x2-adf adjusts
the degrees of freedom by a rule for sparse tables. Their graphs are not equal on any of the 3,183
datasets of child, insurance, alarm, and hepar2 (595 of 790 on asia), and the two tests agree on
97.7% of random triples.

**Reruns that cannot judge.** The moral graph of hepar2 at 1,000 rows is worse in its first run for
IAMB (+5.73) and Inter-IAMB (+4.67) with chi-square tests. Each rerun on seeds 20-29 has 9 paired
datasets, since the datasets on which both packages return an error (a constant column at 1,000
rows) are left out, so it is reported, not judged. Both reruns keep a positive mean D (+4.11 and
+2.00). We have not traced these two settings separately; they involve the same pair of chi-square
tests that differ at 5,000 rows. The two alarm settings that were worse in their first run (IAMB
moral graph with chi-square at 1,000 rows, Inter-IAMB CPDAG with G-square at 5,000 rows) tie on
their reruns.

## Where the authors' RESIT code makes fewer errors

One of the 12 settings of the model is worse in its first run: Gaussian additive noise at 10
variables on standardized columns, where Andrey makes 6.80 mean errors and the authors' code 5.60 (D
+1.20 [+0.05, +2.50], seeds 0-19). The rerun on 20 fresh datasets (seeds 20-39) has the interval
[-0.60, +2.55] around D +0.95 (8.00 and 7.05 mean errors), which includes 0, so the setting counts
as a tie and both runs are in the summary. The orientation ties in both runs. The 40 datasets
together, which was not planned, give +1.08 [+0.13, +2.10].

**RESIT, Gaussian noise, 10 variables.** We traced the gap on the 40 datasets. It comes from the
order in which RESIT tries to remove a variable's parents, which the published algorithm does not
fix: the authors' code takes the columns in their order in the file, and Andrey takes a rank
computed from the data that does not depend on the column order. Several predecessors can fit nearly
as well, so two orders keep different parents. With the authors' column order Andrey returns the
authors' graph on 38 of the 40 datasets and the gap falls to +0.20 [+0.00, +0.50]; 48 random parent
orders moved the mean errors from 0.8 below to 0.8 above the column order's. Whether Andrey's order
is systematically worse than a random one was then tested on 40 datasets not used before (seeds
40-79) with the rule written down in advance: its mean errors are +0.16 [-0.49, +0.85] from those of
16 random orders; 5 of the 16 random orders do as badly or worse (P = 0.35), so it is no worse than
a random order. On those datasets Andrey is +0.68 [-0.53, +1.88] from the authors' code, and a
random order +0.51 [-0.28, +1.26]. What is left of the gap comes from near ties in the choice of the
causal order, where Andrey's smoothing parameters and the authors' GCV minimum differ slightly, as
in CAM. The order is arbitrary in the algorithm, so we do not choose it by its score on these data.

## Method

**Data.** Linear-Gaussian data from Erdos-Renyi graphs with 20, 50, and 100 variables, 1,000 and
5,000 rows, and mean degree 2 and 4, as standardized columns and as columns left in their own units,
and with 200 variables, 1,000 and 5,000 rows, and mean degree 2 and 4, on the outputs whose fits at
100 variables all finished. Each setting has up to 20 datasets (10 at 200 variables), one per seed,
with the columns in a shuffled order. The CPU budget stopped some settings at 10 to 16 datasets. A
setting keeps at least 10, and the ones that bnlearn could not finish keep fewer pairs. The discrete
data are samples of 1,000 and 5,000 rows, up to 20 per setting, from the published networks asia (8
variables), child (20), insurance (27), alarm (37), and hepar2 (70), loaded with
`andrey.data.load_dataset`. A dataset with a constant column is left out when both packages return
an error on it.

**Fits.** One fit at a time on one core, a 600 s cap per fit, and one untimed warm-up fit except at
5,000 rows, mean degree 4, and 50 or more variables, where there is none. Linear-Gaussian data uses
Fisher-z tests at alpha 0.05 (bnlearn `test="zf"`); discrete data uses chi-square and G-square tests
at alpha 0.05 (bnlearn `x2-adf` and `mi-adf`). Andrey's chi-square and G-square tests take as their
degrees of freedom the exact permutation mean of the statistic (Tsamardinos and Borboudakis, 2010);
bnlearn's adjusted tests change the degrees of freedom by a rule for sparse tables. A fit that
reaches the cap is a timeout. It is counted as coverage, left out of the pairs, and never scored as
an empty graph. No discrete-network fit reached the cap. The discrete-network fit times were
measured again with the faster discrete tests of this release, on the same datasets and seeds with
the same protocol; the graphs and errors are those of the first campaign, and the 8,350 comparisons
of the two sets of graphs found no difference.

**Errors.** The structural Hamming distance of the returned graph to its own truth. The tables give
the median over the paired datasets, for Andrey, bnlearn, and the empty graph; D is the mean of the
per-dataset differences, the statistic of the verdict. A DAG that bnlearn returns is compared
through its CPDAG.

**CAM data.** Erdos-Renyi graphs of mean degree 2 with 10, 20, 50, and 100 variables and 1,000 rows,
20 datasets per setting, drawn by `andrey.data.sample_scm` in eight regimes: additive noise with
Gaussian or Gumbel noise, which CAM assumes; location-scale noise, whose scale depends on the
parents; and linear-Gaussian data, which identify only an equivalence class. Each is standardized
(the default) and left in its own units. The columns are in a shuffled order.

**CAM fits.** One fit at a time on one core, a 1,800 s cap per fit, and a short warm-up fit in each
process. CAM 1.0 runs with its preselection and pruning on (`variableSel` and `pruning`, tests at
0.001), which are Andrey's settings. Two differences are named, not tuned away: CAM 1.0's parent cap
above, and smoothing parameters that come from different GCV optimizers, so two edges of nearly
equal gain can be added in either order. Errors are the structural Hamming distance of the returned
DAG, where a reversed edge counts 1, and the share of true edges returned with the right direction.
No fit reached the cap. The reference fits are those of a campaign on the pre-release build; Andrey's
fits were run again on this release's code, on the same datasets and seeds, and the 685 graphs of
CAM from 10 to 100 variables are the campaign's graphs.

**RESIT data.** Erdos-Renyi graphs of mean degree 2 with 5, 10, and 20 variables and 1,000 rows, 20
datasets per setting (10 at 20 variables in their own units, where the CPU budget guard skipped the
rest), drawn by `andrey.data.sample_scm` in the eight regimes of the CAM data, standardized and in
their own units, with the columns in a shuffled order.

**RESIT fits.** One fit at a time on one core, a 1,800 s cap per fit, and a short warm-up fit in
each process. Andrey's `resit` is compared with the code of the authors (Peters, Mooij, Janzing, and
Scholkopf, 2014; R, additive models from `mgcv::gam` with GCV smoothing, an HSIC independence test,
alpha 0.05) and with lingam 1.12.2 (`lingam.RESIT` with a random forest of depth 4, alpha 0.01).
Each package runs its documented settings, and the differences are named, not tuned away. lingam
regresses with a random forest, tests at alpha 0.01, and takes as the next sink the variable with
the smallest HSIC statistic, where Andrey and the authors' code take the largest p-value. The
authors' code removes a variable's parents in the order of the columns, where Andrey removes them in
a rank computed from the data, and it compares p-values themselves, so p-values below the smallest
double tie at 0. The algorithm of the paper fixes neither order. Errors are the structural Hamming
distance of the returned DAG, where a reversed edge counts 1, and the share of true edges returned
with the right direction. No fit reached the cap. The reference fits are those of a campaign on a
pre-release build; Andrey's fits were run again on this release's code, on the same datasets and
seeds.

**Rerun.** A worse setting is rerun once on fresh seeds (20 to 29 for the other methods and 20 to 39
for RESIT, which has 20 datasets per setting), with the same build and arms. The verdict of the
rerun is its own interval.

## Speed

## Limits

The linear-Gaussian settings cover 20 to 200 variables and 1,000 to 5,000 rows with Fisher-z tests;
the discrete settings cover five networks with up to 70 variables and the same two sample sizes.
Other data types, other tests, and larger discrete networks are not measured here. At 100 variables,
1,000 rows, and mean degree 2 on unstandardized data, the mean CPDAG of IAMB makes 79 errors against
100 for the empty graph, the closest of any setting (bnlearn: 80); on standardized data it is 20
variables, 1,000 rows, and mean degree 4, with 24 against 40.

Several CPDAG settings have fewer than 10 paired datasets and are not judged, because bnlearn
reached the cap: IAMB at 100 variables, 5,000 rows, and mean degree 4 on standardized data (9 pairs,
11 caps) and at 200 variables, 1,000 rows, and mean degree 4 (9 pairs, 1 cap). MMPC's CPDAG has 2
such settings on standardized data (100 variables and mean degree 4, where Andrey reached the cap in
6 and 19 fits and bnlearn in 18 and 20), 6 on unstandardized data, and 1 at 200 variables (5 pairs).
The MMPC skeleton at 200 variables and 5,000 rows is the one MMPC setting worse than bnlearn's on
two runs. Two discrete settings of RSMAX2 at insurance and 1,000 rows have 8 pairs and are not
judged. At 200 variables the skeleton and CPDAG of 5,000 rows and mean degree 4 are not measured,
because bnlearn did not finish all fits at 100 variables there.

CAM's settings cover 10 to 100 variables at 1,000 rows. Location-scale noise and linear-Gaussian
data lie outside its model and are reported, not judged: there CAM returns 77% to 81% (location-scale
noise) and 61% to 64% (linear Gaussian) of the true edges with the right direction, and linear-Gaussian
data identify only the equivalence class. CAM makes fewer errors than the empty graph in all 32
settings, and so does CAM 1.0. Larger graphs, more rows, hidden common causes, and effects that
interact are not measured here.

RESIT's settings cover 5 to 20 variables at 1,000 rows. Its accuracy claim holds up to 10 variables.
At 20 variables with Gaussian additive noise no implementation clearly beats the empty graph: Andrey
makes 19.4 mean errors, the authors' code 20.2, and lingam 44.8, against 20.0 for the empty graph
(19.8 for Andrey on columns in their own units, 10 datasets), so 20 variables is a range for speed.
With Gumbel noise at 20 variables Andrey (15.6) and the authors' code (14.5) are below the empty
graph and lingam (46.1) is not. Location-scale noise and linear-Gaussian data lie outside RESIT's
model and are reported, not judged: there RESIT returns 25% to 33% (location-scale noise) and 41% to
46% (linear Gaussian) of the true edges with the right direction, and its mean errors are above the
empty graph's in all 6 location-scale settings and in 4 of the 6 linear-Gaussian settings. Larger
graphs, more rows, hidden common causes, and effects that interact are not measured here.

You can find environment setup, campaign drivers, and scores in the [benchmark harness

the authors' RESIT code with R 4.6.1 in the harness's R environment, and lingam 1.12.2. Every driver
shuffles the columns by default. Times will differ with the hardware and build; graphs should match
closely.

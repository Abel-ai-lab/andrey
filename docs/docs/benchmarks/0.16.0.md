0.16.0 takes three earlier themes further. For missing data, you can simulate values that go
missing for a known reason, correct PC when missingness depends on other variables with MVPC, and
fit the score searches, NOTEARS, DAGMA, and CALM on incomplete data with EM. Background knowledge
can now say that one variable causes another by some path, that it does not, or that two share a
hidden common cause. LOCI reads the direction of a pair whose noise grows or shrinks with the cause.

Drawings also mark intervened variables as causal-discovery figures do, every method that tests with
HSIC keeps its p-values precise far below 1e-16, and FCI's documentation says when a fit is not a
PAG. LOCI and MVPC are supported, and so is EM in NOTEARS, hill climbing, and Tabu search; EM in the
other searches is experimental. The new knowledge parts are supported in PC, GES, BOSS, FCI, GFCI,
BOSS-FCI, LPCMCI, and DirectLiNGAM, and experimental in the other methods.

## Missing data

Values go missing for reasons, and the reason decides which method you can trust. 0.16.0 lets you
simulate those reasons and gives you two methods that correct for them. The
[Ready for real data post](../../blog/20261029-andrey-0-11-0.md) now describes them beside test-wise
deletion on a real survey, and [Missing values](../guides/getting-started.md#missing-values) in the
getting-started guide explains each one.

### Simulated missingness

`andrey.data.missingness` draws missing values whose missingness graph you know: `mcar`, missing
completely at random; `mar`, where a column's missingness depends on columns that are always
observed; `mnar`, where it depends on other columns that can go missing; and `self_masking`, where
it depends on the column's own value. Each follows a logistic form (Muzellec et al., 2020) or the
quantile form of the MVPC simulations (Tu et al., 2019), and `parents=` places each missingness
indicator. Pass one as `missing=` to `sample_scm` or `SCM.sample`, which draws the mask from the
seed's own stream, so the same seed without it gives the complete data; `missingness.draw` masks any
complete data. `CausalDataset.missing` holds the mask and the parents of each indicator, so you can
check a method against the truth.

### One argument for missing values

Every method that accepts missing values names its handling with one argument, `missing`. PC, FCI,
CD-NOD, FCI-JCI, IAMB, Inter-IAMB, MMPC, PCMCI, PCMCI+, LPCMCI, ANM, PNL, and LOCI take
`missing="testwise"`, test-wise deletion. The default, `None`, keeps each method's own handling, so
no result changes, and `metadata["missing"]` records the handling a fit used. A value a method does
not list raises a `ValueError` that names the values it takes.

### MVPC

`pc(data, missing="mvpc")` runs Missing-Value PC (Tu et al., 2019) with its permutation-based
correction. After the test-wise skeleton, it finds the parents of each column's missingness
indicator, then tests again, on virtual data that correct for them, the edges such an indicator
could have kept. `metadata["missingness_parents"]` and `metadata["corrected_edges"]`
record what it found. A corrected test with too few rows where its variables and those parents are
all observed is answered by the test-wise test, and `corrected_edges["uncorrected"]` lists its pair.
On complete data, it gives the test-wise result.

MVPC corrects `"fisherz"` and `"student_t"`. Its correction holds when no missingness indicator
causes a variable, every independence in the observed rows also holds in the missing ones, no
indicator is a deterministic function of others, and no value causes its own missingness; the data
cannot check these. The permutation correction also needs linear-Gaussian variables and the
paper's two conditions: (i) given `W`, the parents of the indicators involved, those indicators
are independent of the tested variables; and (ii) conditioning on the indicators of `W` leaves the
tested relation unchanged. Condition (ii) fails when such an indicator is a common effect of the
tested pair, as in the paper's Figure 1c, and the corrected test can then keep an extra edge. The
paper's density-ratio correction for those cases is not implemented.

### EM

GES, XGES, hill climbing, Tabu search, exact search, BOSS, and GRaSP with `score_func="bic"`, and
NOTEARS, DAGMA, and CALM read the data only through their covariance, so they cannot delete rows per
test. `missing="em"` fits them by structural EM (Friedman, 1997; Gao et al., 2022). Under a
linear-Gaussian model of the current graph, it computes the expected covariance of the complete
data, runs the method's own search on it, and fits the model again to the graph it returns, for 10
rounds. `metadata["em"]` records the observed-data log-likelihood after each round.

EM is sound when values are missing completely at random or at random given the observed values;
when whether a value is missing depends on the value itself, it has no guarantee. It takes
`score_func="bic"` only, and no `env`. Without it, these methods refuse missing values with a
message that names `missing="em"`.

### What we measured on missing data

We measured both corrections on simulated data, each against a reference implementation and
against four baselines fit on the same files: test-wise PC, the method after dropping every
incomplete row, the method after filling in column means, and the empty graph. The
[comparison page][md] has every lane and number, from 192,700 fits.

A cell is a graph, a sample size, a reason for values to go missing, and a missing rate, with 20 or
50 datasets. A method is better or worse than another in a cell when the 95% bootstrap interval of
their paired difference in errors (SHD) lies wholly on one side of zero, and a worse cell counts
only when it is worse again on 20 fresh datasets. A supported value is never less accurate than
its reference in a cell that repeats, and is below the empty graph in the setting it claims.

| Method | Reference | Cells better | Tied | Worse | Below the empty graph | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `pc(missing="mvpc")` | The MVPC authors' R code | 28 of 32 | 4 | 0 | 32 of 32 | Supported |
| `notears(missing="em")` | MissDAG | 19 of 24 | 5 | 0 | 22 of 24 | Supported |
| `hc(missing="em")` | bnlearn's structural EM | 29 of 36 | 7 | 0 | 23 of 36 | Supported |
| `tabu(missing="em")` | bnlearn's structural EM | 32 of 36 | 4 | 0 | 27 of 36 | Supported |

We ran MVPC beside its authors' R code in the paper's setting: random graphs of 20 and 50
variables, 100 to 10,000 rows, values missing at random and not at random, and for each a control
with as many values missing completely at random. MVPC is below the empty graph in all 32 cells.
Where values go missing for a reason it makes fewer errors than test-wise PC in 10 of 16 cells and
ties in 6, and in the controls it ties, as it should: at 50 variables and 10,000 rows with values
missing at random, it makes 5.1 errors against test-wise PC's 14.3, in 2.5 s against the R code's
85.5 s. The two packages' corrections agree: their skeletons tie in 30 of the 32 cells and differ
by at most 0.004 in F1 in the other two. The margin over the R code comes from PC's orientation
step, where Andrey ranks conflicting colliders by p-value.

On a wider grid of our own, 10 to 100 variables and 500 to 10,000 rows, MVPC is better than
test-wise PC under the paper's missing-at-random mask in 14 of 72 cells at `alpha=0.01` and 28 at
`alpha=0.05`, and worse in none. It does not help when each missingness indicator depends weakly on
many variables: there it ties in 125 of 141 cells. With 50 or 100 variables and 20 or 30% of the
values missing, MVPC can be behind test-wise PC. Over both levels, it is behind on the first
datasets and again on 20 fresh ones in 12 cells, by 0.7 to 3.6 errors: 9 under the paper's
missing-not-at-random mask and 3 where each indicator depends weakly on many variables; the
[comparison page][md] lists them. In three of those cells we also ran the MVPC authors' R code on
the same fresh datasets: its MVPC tied its own test-wise PC, and Andrey's MVPC was no less accurate
than it, so there the correction helps in neither implementation and test-wise deletion is the
better choice. No cell with values missing completely at random repeats. With many weak parents, 100
variables, and 10,000 rows, the search for the indicators' parents is also slow, and 77 fits ran
past the cap of 30 minutes; `max_depth` bounds it. A corrected test with too few rows falls back to
the test-wise test, which happened in 962 of 14,323 fits, and `corrected_edges["uncorrected"]` lists
each such pair.

NOTEARS with EM ran beside MissDAG, the code of Gao et al. (2022), in that paper's setting: 20
variables, 100 rows, and 10 to 50% of the values missing. It is better in 19 of 24 cells and ties
in 5, and its median fit took 8 to 42 s against 49 to 114 s. Part of the margin is NOTEARS itself,
which is ahead by 0.85 to 1.8 errors on the complete data. With half of the values missing
completely at random on the two denser graphs, neither package is below the empty graph.

Hill climbing and Tabu search with EM ran beside bnlearn's `structural.em` with the same search
inside, both on the plain BIC. Neither is worse in any cell, and both are well ahead as the
missing rate grows: under values missing completely at random on graphs of mean degree 2, bnlearn's
errors rise from 12.4 to 30.1 between 10 and 50% missing, where Andrey's hill climbing goes from
12.0 to 15.2. With 20 variables and 500 or 2,000 rows, both searches are below the empty graph in
12 of 12 cells. With 15 variables and 100 rows the result is often no better than the empty graph:
hill climbing is below it in 11 of 24 cells and Tabu search in 15. The plain search at that sample
size is the cause: on complete data from the sparsest graphs it is behind the empty graph too, with
8.8 errors for hill climbing and 8.45 for Tabu search against 8.0. Both searches do well with EM
from 500 rows on.

GES, XGES, BOSS, GRaSP, exact search, CALM, and DAGMA with EM stay experimental, because no
reference implementation exists to compare them with: MissDAG's release fits NOTEARS only, and
bnlearn's structural EM takes hill climbing and Tabu search. Their results against the baselines
are on the comparison page. On the wider grid, BOSS and GRaSP with EM were never worse than
test-wise PC, dropping rows, or mean imputation, and were below the empty graph in 214 and 213 of
216 cells. With 100 rows and 40 to 50% of the values missing, GES, BOSS, and GRaSP return almost
the empty graph at the default penalty.

## Path and confounder claims

`andrey.Knowledge` takes three more parts. `required_paths` lists the pairs
`(a, b)` in which `a` causes `b`, directly or through other variables; `forbidden_paths`, the pairs
in which it does not; and `confounded`, the pairs that share a hidden common cause, a variable that
is not in the data.

```python
knowledge = andrey.Knowledge(
    required_paths=[("smoking", "cancer")],  # directly or through other variables
    confounded=[("diet", "exercise")],  # a hidden common cause
)
```

- **CPDAG methods.** PC, CD-NOD, GES, XGES, hill climbing, Tabu search, MMHC, RSMAX2, BOSS, GRaSP,
  exact search, and IAMB, Inter-IAMB, and MMPC with `structure="cpdag"` honor the two path parts.
  Of the DAGs the found graph stands for, they keep those that meet every claim and return their
  MPDAG (Fang, Zhao, Liu, and He, 2025). `metadata["ancestor_clauses"]` lists the claims that no
  MPDAG can show. The result is the class the search found, restricted, not a new search for the
  best class that meets the claims.
- **PAG methods.** FCI, FCI-JCI, GFCI, BOSS-FCI, GRaSP-FCI, and LPCMCI honor all three parts. On a
  pair the tests keep, a required path makes the edge `a -> b` and a forbidden path puts an
  arrowhead at its cause, and no test removes a `confounded` pair. These marks and checks assume no
  selection bias. LPCMCI takes each claim with its lag.
- **DirectLiNGAM** and multi-group DirectLiNGAM read the two path parts to narrow the causal order
  (Shimizu et al., 2011).

A claim the graph a fit finds does not meet is dropped: the fit warns with `KnowledgeWarning` and
lists it in `metadata["unmet_knowledge"]`.

Knowledge that contradicts itself raises a `ValueError` before the search, and a method given a
part it does not honor raises `NotImplementedError`, naming the methods that do.
[Background knowledge](../guides/getting-started.md#background-knowledge) in the getting-started
guide shows each part, and the [Ready for real data post](../../blog/20261029-andrey-0-11-0.md)
uses them on a survey.

### What we measured

We checked them three ways: against exact answers, against the same fit without the claims, and
against reference packages given the same knowledge. The [comparison page][kp] has every lane and
number. With d-separation in place of the tests, the CPDAG step gave the exact answer on all 131,296
claim sets over 32,824 graphs, and every mark that FCI, GFCI, and BOSS-FCI set from true claims was
the true graph's, on 14,946 fits each.

On simulated data we gave each method true claims, one part at a time and all together, on 5 to
30% of the variable pairs, and counted its errors against the truth with the claims and without,
on 20 datasets per cell. A cell counts as worse only when it is worse again on 20 fresh datasets.
Every fit returned a graph. A CPDAG method cannot direct every edge, so we score PC, GES, and BOSS
against what a method told the claims can know, the true graph's class narrowed by them, and also
by the distance to the true DAG; a cell worse again in either would count.

| Method | Parts | Cells worse with true claims | Cells better | Claims dropped | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FCI | all three | 0 of 24 | 6 | 0 to 9.7% | Supported |
| GFCI | all three | 0 of 24 | 6 | 0 to 7.9% | Supported |
| BOSS-FCI | all three | 0 of 24 | 6 | 0 to 7.0% | Supported |
| LPCMCI | all three | 0 of 24 | 10 | 0 to 2.9% | Supported |
| DirectLiNGAM | two path parts | 0 of 24 | 1 | 0 to 0.6% | Supported |
| PC | two path parts | 0 of 72 | 65 | 23.8 to 59.9% | Supported |
| GES | two path parts | 0 of 36 | 36 | 1.3 to 7.0% | Supported |
| BOSS | two path parts | 0 of 72 | 72 | 0.8 to 2.9% | Supported |

A cell here is a graph size, a sample size, and a share of pairs claimed, with one part; Claims
dropped is the share of the true claims a method dropped because the graph it found does not meet
them, for its lowest and highest part. In FCI, GFCI, BOSS-FCI, and LPCMCI a required path lowered
the errors in 18 of 32 cells, and no fit dropped a `confounded` pair. DirectLiNGAM with the same
claims was no less accurate than lingam's in any cell, and fit the 140 of 240 datasets with
forbidden paths on which lingam raised an error.

The Status column also reflects the reference packages. We ran Tetrad's PC, FGES, BOSS, FCI, GFCI,
and BOSS-FCI, pcalg's PC, tigramite's LPCMCI, and lingam's DirectLiNGAM on the same datasets with
the same knowledge. tigramite and lingam take the claims themselves. Tetrad and pcalg take edges,
not paths, so we gave them the edges the claims imply and gave Andrey's methods the same edges. With
the same knowledge, no method was less accurate than its reference in a cell that repeated, and none
was without knowledge. That comparison decides the status: each of the eight methods above supports
every part it honors.

The comparison page also shows a second comparison, which is not like for like: Andrey given a
required path against a reference given the required edge that path implies. The edge says more. It
also tells the reference that the pair is adjacent, which the benchmark reads from the true graph,
while a path says only that one variable is an ancestor of the other and cannot restore an edge the
search left out. There PC, GES with the plain BIC, FCI, and GFCI given the paths made more errors
than their references given the edges, in cells that repeated, and BOSS in one cell with all parts
together; with forbidden paths none did. So when you know the edge itself, state it as `required`:
a path claim says less and directs less.

No reference takes a path claim between variables that are not adjacent (58% of the required paths
for the CPDAG methods, 32% for the PAG methods) or a hidden common cause in Tetrad's knowledge, so
for those the evidence is the first two checks.

For PC, GES, and BOSS, Cells better counts the cells closer to the true DAG with the claims: with
them a graph was up to 15 edges closer to it. The CPDAG methods restrict the graph the search found,
and their rules take its class as given. On finite data that graph can be wrong, and a true claim
then directs some edges the wrong way: 5.8 to 10.9% of the edges the claims directed with PC, 1.7 to
4.1% with BOSS, and 1.1 to 1.7% with GES, every one in a fit whose graph had an edge missing or
extra or a collider wrong. GES is judged on the sparser graphs, at mean degree 2: at mean degree 4,
outside the setting it suits, its fit with the default settings did not stay below the empty graph
with or without the claims, and no cell there was worse with them. We have not measured the parts
in the methods this benchmark does not run, where they are experimental: CD-NOD, XGES, hill
climbing, Tabu search, MMHC, RSMAX2, GRaSP, exact search, IAMB, Inter-IAMB, MMPC, FCI-JCI,
GRaSP-FCI, and multi-group DirectLiNGAM.

## LOCI

`andrey.loci` decides the direction of a variable pair under the location-scale noise model of Immer
et al. (2023), `y = f(x) + g(x) n`, in which the spread of the effect changes with the cause. In
each direction, it fits a Gaussian whose mean and variance are spline functions of the proposed
cause. It decides by an HSIC test of the scaled residual against the cause,
`criterion="independence"`, the default, or by the larger likelihood, `criterion="likelihood"`. Like
ANM and PNL, it reads the rows where both columns are observed and takes `missing`.

LOCI is supported. We compared each criterion with the same criterion of the LOCI authors' code,
LOCI-H for independence and LOCI-M for likelihood, on 100 pairs in each of 26 settings. Neither
criterion made significantly more errors than the authors' code in any setting we judged, 39
comparisons in all. On 8 of the 10 location-scale comparisons we ran again on 100 fresh pairs it
made fewer errors both times (5 with the default criterion, 3 with the likelihood). Andrey's median
fit was 40 to 912 times faster with the default criterion and 376 to 1,948 times faster with the
likelihood. The [comparison page][loci] lists every row.

- The settings: location-scale pairs with Gaussian, Gumbel, or Laplace noise at 500, 1,000, and
  2,000 rows, each also with the cause rescaled to unit variance; additive-noise pairs with Gaussian
  noise at the same sizes; and the five synthetic sets of Tagasovska et al. (2020) at 1,000 rows,
  drawn from that paper's description.
- The independence criterion is judged in all 26 settings. The likelihood criterion fits a Gaussian
  model, so it is judged in the 13 settings with Gaussian noise and reported in the others.
- We count the pairs whose direction a fit names wrong. A comparison counts as fewer errors only
  when it comes out so again on 100 fresh pairs; we reran the location-scale settings with Gaussian
  noise that came out ahead on the first pairs, and every other comparison is reported as no less
  accurate.
- On 11 of the 2,600 pairs, all with Gumbel or Laplace noise, the build we first ran raised an
  error; 0.16.0 fits them (the solver fix in the [changelog](../changelog.md)), and the table holds
  its results.

Pairs with the true direction, of 100, at 1,000 rows:

| Location-scale, Gaussian noise | 94 | 87 | 100 | 99 |
| Location-scale, Laplace noise | 91 | 83 | 44\* | 35\* |
| Location-scale, Gumbel noise | 95 | 79 | 37\* | 35\* |
| Additive, Gaussian noise | 90 | 89 | 93 | 87 |

to 38 pairs of 100 right at every size, fewer than a coin flip, and the authors' LOCI-M 31 to 37. So
keep the default criterion unless you know the noise is Gaussian: the independence criterion named
most pairs right in all 26 settings, 79 to 100 of 100.

LOCI decides one pair; for more variables, use CAM. The cause must range without bounds, so a
uniform cause is outside its model. Under `criterion="independence"`, the HSIC test's time and
memory grow with the square of the number of rows: on 20 pairs the median fit took 60 s and 10 GB at
16,000 rows, and at 32,000 rows every fit ran past a 28 GB cap, where the likelihood criterion took
1.4 s. The [pairwise direction post](../../blog/20261015-andrey-0-4-0.md) describes LOCI beside ANM
and PNL.

## Intervention marks

`andrey.viz.draw(structure, targets=[...])` draws each intervention target as a square, as before,
and now marks each edge with an arrowhead into a target by a cross in its middle, where 0.15.0 put
a bar by the arrowhead. A target that no arrowhead points into is marked by its square alone, where
0.15.0 drew a short barred arrow into it from outside. A hard intervention cuts the edges into its
targets and a soft one keeps them; both get the same marks, so a cross does not mean the edge was
removed.

`andrey.viz.to_graphviz(structure, targets=[...])` draws the targets too, as `square` nodes, named
by label or by position. It leaves out the cross because Graphviz marks only an edge's ends. The
graph types guide has a new section, [How to read a causal
graph](../guides/graph-types.md#how-to-read-a-causal-graph), with a legend of every mark `draw`
uses.

## HSIC p-values

ANM, PNL, RCD, BottomUpParceLiNGAM, GIN, and `indep_test="kci"` without conditions compute their
HSIC p-values from the upper tail of the gamma approximation through its logarithm, as RESIT and
CAM-UV already did. A p-value no longer rounds to 0 below about 1e-16; it reads 0 only below about
1e-308. ANM and PNL compare the logarithms of their two p-values, which keep their order even
there, so their HSIC statistics decide only between equal p-values. BottomUpParceLiNGAM and GIN
pool a finite logarithm by Fisher's method where they pooled infinity or the smallest double.

A result can therefore change where both p-values were below 1e-16 and the HSIC statistics
decided. On 970 pairs drawn as the ANM and PNL benchmarks draw theirs, from 200 to 16,000 rows, no
direction changed. A statistic that rounding puts just below 0 gives a p-value of 1 in RCD,
BottomUpParceLiNGAM, and GIN, where it gave `nan`. The [pairwise direction
post](../../blog/20261015-andrey-0-4-0.md) now describes 0.16.0's rule.

## FCI fits that are not PAGs

FCI returns the same graphs as in 0.15.0, and its documentation now says what they can be. On
finite data, test errors can give separating sets that no maximal ancestral graph (MAG) fits, and
the `pag` that FCI returns is then not the PAG of any MAG. The **Limitations** paragraph of
{py:func}`~andrey.fci` gives how often: of 200 simulated datasets with 10 variables, mean degree 3,
and 2,000 rows, 36 fits with `"fisherz"` and 36 with `"normal_scores"` returned one. On 28 and 29 of
them, the MAG that Zhang's construction builds from the `pag` is not ancestral, and a graph query
such as `m_separated` raised `ValueError`.

{py:func}`~andrey.bootstrap` already passes over such a fit: when the vote of the fits has no valid
PAG, its averaged graph is the valid resample graph nearest the others. Its Notes now name an FCI
fit that is not a PAG among the graphs it passes over.

## J-PCMCI+ and a shared pulse

J-PCMCI+ now tells you when a pulse that every dataset shares is left out of the time contexts.
When a system variable has a copy among a test's conditions, `jpcmciplus` raises
`SingularCorrelationError` with the note to list it as a time context, where it dropped that
variable's links without an error. [Hank Zhu](https://github.com/HouZhu2001) found and fixed it
([#841](https://github.com/Abel-ai-lab/andrey/pull/841)). Thank you, Hank.
